\begin{abstract}
Fixed-time signal plans remain the default in many South Asian cities, where traffic is motorcycle-dominated,
demand is strongly asymmetric between approaches, and a detector plant is unaffordable. We present \relay, an
adaptive signal controller whose only input is one existing fixed junction camera. A single convolutional
detector yields per-approach, per-class counts, converted to passenger-car-unit (\pcu) demand and consumed by
a queue-weighted pressure controller in the max-pressure family, with the operational rules a deployable
signal needs: empty-phase skipping, gap-out, minimum and maximum green, a hard anti-starvation bound,
pedestrian demand treated as pressure, and emergency preemption with a bounded hold. The controller
estimates each approach's arrival and discharge rate from the counts themselves, with no survey and no
configured saturation flow, and uses them to size each green from Webster's cycle on the measured degree of
saturation and to price a phase change at the discharge its clearance forfeits. We evaluate against
equal-split and demand-optimal Webster fixed plans, plain max-pressure, self-organising traffic lights and
vehicle-actuated control in a seeded point-queue simulation, and against Webster, max-pressure and gap-actuated
control in SUMO with a motorcycle-dominated fleet and a sublane model. In the point-queue grid of
\GridCells{} cells across seven demand shapes, two topologies and loads to \GridDemandHigh{}~veh/h, mean
delay falls against the Webster plan in every cell but one, by up to \ExpOneMaxWebster\%, and against
actuated control in \PolActuatedWins{} of \GridCells{} cells; the worst cell is analysed and traced to lost
time near capacity rather than to the fairness bound. In SUMO the controller reduces delay in every one of
\SumoWebsterRuns{} paired runs against each baseline, by \SumoWebsterMin\% to \SumoWebsterMax\% against Webster and
\SumoActuatedMin\% to \SumoActuatedMax\% against actuated control, although at the highest load its worst
observed wait is longer than the fixed plans'. A corridor with finite links in SUMO confirms the point-queue
finding that the downstream coupling term contributes at most \ScorrCoupleAbsMax\% of delay. The controller is
unaffected by the biased, density-dependent undercount the detector actually exhibits, and emergency
preemption reaches green in \PreMeanRelay~s on average over \PreTrials{} trials with a worst case of
\PreWorstRelay~s against a closed-form bound of 14.0~s. This is a simulation and real-footage study, not a
field deployment.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

\IEEEPARstart{U}{rban} signal control in low- and middle-income cities is usually performed by fixed-time
plans or by police officers operating signals by hand. Both allocate green without measuring what is queued.
The consequence is visible at almost any asymmetric junction: a busy arterial approach holds a long queue
while the crossing minor street receives the green it would receive at three in the morning. Webster's
method~\cite{Webster1958TrafficSignalSettings} showed that cycle length and splits should follow demand, but a
fixed plan can only follow the demand measured when it was designed, and re-timing surveys are infrequent.
Studies of signalised intersections in the Kathmandu valley report saturation flows and delays that differ
substantially from standard capacity guidance, attributing the difference to the local vehicle mixture and to
lane discipline~\cite{shrestha2014development,nepali2024assessment}, which is exactly the condition under
which a plan imported from a design manual performs badly.

The established remedy is a demand-responsive system such as SCOOT~\cite{Hunt1981SCOOT,Robertson1991SCOOTMethod}
or SCATS~\cite{Sims1980SCATS}, or an optimisation-based controller in the OPAC~\cite{Gartner1983OPAC} and
RHODES~\cite{Mirchandani2001RHODES} lineage. These work and have decades of field evidence, but they assume a
detector plant, communications and a control room, so per-junction cost is dominated by sensing and civil
works rather than by the algorithm. In a city where the signal head itself is sometimes unpowered, that
capital profile is prohibitive.

Two conditions have changed. Fixed cameras are already installed at many urban junctions for enforcement and
monitoring, so a camera-based controller reuses a paid-for asset. And single-stage object detectors are now
accurate and cheap enough to run at video rate on commodity hardware~\cite{redmon2016yolo,bochkovskiy2020yolov4},
which makes per-approach counting a software problem rather than a procurement problem. Vision-based queue
and density estimation from fixed cameras is established in its own
right~\cite{umair2021efficient,harilakshmi2023deep,abewickrema2025ensemble}, and several recent studies connect
a detector to a signal timer on this basis~\cite{papanashi2024adaptive,alruyshid2025aibased,talaat2025smart,medvei2025deepsignal}.

What is often missing in that applied literature is the control side. Many camera-based studies compute a
count and set green in proportion to it, which is a proportional rule rather than a control policy, and they
rarely state what prevents a minor approach from being starved, what happens when the detector fails, or how
the policy behaves at saturation. They are also usually compared only against a fixed-time plan, which an
adaptive controller of any competence will beat. Conversely, the theoretical literature on max-pressure
control~\cite{Varaiya2013MaxPressure,Varaiya2013MaxPressureChapter} provides throughput-optimality results in
a form that assumes ideal queue observation and imposes no minimum green, no clearance interval and no
fairness bound, all of which a deployable signal requires.

This paper describes and evaluates \relay, a controller placed between those positions. Its contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
\item A single-camera perception-to-control pipeline for heterogeneous traffic in which presence and
\pcu-weighted demand are kept as separate quantities, because a \pcu-weighted score alone classifies a lone
motorcycle as an empty lane.
\item A self-calibrating pressure controller. Arrival and discharge rates are estimated per approach from the
count sequence alone (Section~\ref{sec:estimation}) and used for two decisions a constant cannot make: each
stage's green is floored at its share of Webster's cycle on the measured degree of saturation
(Section~\ref{sec:floor}), and a stage change must beat the discharge its clearance interval forfeits
(Section~\ref{sec:cost}). No saturation-flow table, survey or per-site constant is required. Without these an
unconstrained pressure rule terminates every green at the safety minimum once demand approaches capacity and
loses to a fixed plan outright.
\item Fairness enforced by explicit bounds rather than by the score: separate maximum-wait bounds for vehicles
and pedestrians, a cap on the preemption hold, and an anti-starvation rule that serves the worst-treated queue
instead of rotating among equally starved ones.
\item An evaluation that reports where the controller loses. It is compared against a Webster plan computed
from the true mean demand, against three adaptive baselines (plain max-pressure, self-organising traffic
lights and vehicle-actuated control), per cell over a \GridCells-cell grid, and in SUMO with a
motorcycle-dominated fleet, turning movements and finite links. The count error the detector actually makes,
a biased density-dependent undercount, is modelled rather than replaced by convenient zero-mean noise, and the
sensitivity to the two parameters the design depends on most is measured.
\end{itemize}

We are explicit about what this is not. There is no field installation, no controller cabinet in the loop and
no before-and-after study on a real signal: control results come from seeded simulation and perception results
from recorded footage. The contribution is a design and feasibility study at the level a city could use to
decide whether a pilot is worth funding, and Section~\ref{sec:limitations} states which claims a field trial
would still have to establish.

\section{Related Work}

\subsection{Fixed-time, actuated and demand-responsive control}

Webster's analysis of cycle length and green splits~\cite{Webster1958TrafficSignalSettings} remains the
reference method for fixed-time plans and is the basis of the stronger fixed baseline used here.
Vehicle-actuated control, in which a green is extended by a passage time on each detected arrival until a gap
or a maximum is reached, is the most widely deployed adaptive scheme and is documented in signal timing
practice~\cite{koonce2008timing}; it is our strongest adaptive baseline. Among demand-responsive systems, SCOOT
adjusts splits, offsets and cycle time from cyclic flow profiles measured upstream of the stop line, SCATS
selects among plans using measured degree of saturation, and OPAC and RHODES solve a rolling-horizon
optimisation over predicted arrivals. All depend on detectors installed and maintained in the carriageway, and
all were designed for streams with lane discipline and similar vehicle sizes.

\subsection{Max-pressure and self-organising control}

Max-pressure signal control derives from backpressure scheduling in queueing networks~\cite{Tassiulas1992Stability}.
Varaiya~\cite{Varaiya2013MaxPressure,Varaiya2013MaxPressureChapter} showed that repeatedly activating the phase
maximising a weighted difference between upstream and downstream queues stabilises any demand within the
capacity region using only local queue information, which is attractive for a decentralised deployment.
Related work established distributed formulations~\cite{Wongpiromsarn2012Distributed} and decentralised
variants with weaker information requirements~\cite{Le2015Decentralized}. Two properties of the theoretical
policy conflict with practice. The argmax is evaluated freely at every decision epoch, so the policy may switch
as often as the decision interval allows, and each switch costs a clearance interval during which no approach
discharges; Levin et al.~\cite{Levin2020CyclicalMaxPressure} address this with a cyclical phase structure, and
our saturated-demand results are an independent, quantified instance of the same effect. And throughput
optimality is proved for the unconstrained policy; adding minimum green, maximum green, hysteresis and forced
service breaks the argmax property and voids the proof. We therefore describe our controller as a
queue-weighted pressure heuristic in the max-pressure family and make no stability claim.

Self-organising traffic lights~\cite{gershenson2005sotl,cools2013sotl} are a second decentralised rule that
needs only local counts: a red phase accumulates waiting vehicles times seconds and takes green when the
integral passes a threshold, unless the current green is discharging a short platoon. It is included as a
baseline because it shares our information requirements and is simpler than our rule.

\subsection{Learning-based control}

Reinforcement learning is the dominant research direction for signal control. Surveys by Wei et
al.~\cite{wei2021recent}, Haydari and Yilmaz~\cite{haydari2022deep} and Noaeen et al.~\cite{noaeen2022reinforcement}
cover the field; representative works include IntelliLight~\cite{wei2018intellilight}, large-scale
decentralised training~\cite{chen2020thousand} and PressLight~\cite{wei2019presslight}, whose reward derives
from max-pressure. Genders and Razavi~\cite{genders2016using} established the basic formulation. We did not
adopt a learned policy, for reasons specific to the deployment context: training requires a simulator
calibrated to the target junction and none exists for the junctions we are concerned with; a neural policy
does not naturally expose the maximum-wait or clearance guarantees an authority approving a signal plan
needs; and it transfers poorly across geometries, whereas a pressure rule parameterised only by a phase set
moves to a new junction by drawing polygons on a camera image. The comparison we do not make, and which should
be made, is a head-to-head against a trained policy on the same arrival streams.

\subsection{Camera-based counting and vision-driven signal control}

Vehicle detection from fixed cameras is a mature application of single-stage
detectors~\cite{redmon2016yolo,bochkovskiy2020yolov4}, reviewed by Terven et al.~\cite{terven2023comprehensive},
typically trained on general corpora~\cite{lin2014microsoft} and extended with tracking when trajectories are
required~\cite{bewley2016simple,wojke2017simple}. Our system uses the Ultralytics implementation of the YOLO
family~\cite{jocher2023yolov8}; the architecture generation we fine-tune is described
in~\cite{khanam2024yolov11}, which remains a preprint. Because the controller consumes instantaneous
per-approach occupancy rather than cumulative crossings, we count detections inside approach polygons instead
of tracking vehicles, which removes identity-switch errors in dense queues from the control path.

Recent applied studies pair such a detector with a signal timer. Papanashi et al.~\cite{papanashi2024adaptive}
report YOLO-based adaptive timing evaluated in Bengaluru, a heterogeneous-traffic context comparable to ours;
Alruyshid et al.~\cite{alruyshid2025aibased} add emergency and bus prioritisation in a Baghdad case study;
Talaat et al.~\cite{talaat2025smart} and Medvei et al.~\cite{medvei2025deepsignal} report detector-driven
controllers, the latter combining detection with a learned policy. These works establish that the perception
component is practical. Our contribution relative to them is on the control and evaluation side: an explicit
pressure formulation, stated fairness bounds, a preemption latency bound, and an evaluation against
demand-optimal fixed-time and actuated baselines that reports where the adaptive policy loses.

Emergency preemption has a long operational history and a careful literature on its side effects, since a
preemption interrupts the cycle and can create hazards at the transition~\cite{louisell2004simple}; Humagain et
al.~\cite{humagain2020systematic} review route optimisation and preemption methods, and recent work exploits
connected-vehicle messaging to grant priority with explicit dilemma-zone protection~\cite{das2022traffic}. Our
detection channel differs: the emergency vehicle is recognised visually by the same detector that counts
ordinary traffic and no vehicle-side equipment is required. That is an advantage in cost and a disadvantage in
reliability, which is why the ambulance class carries the highest confidence threshold and why the hold is
capped, and what we add is a worst-case latency bound with a measurement against it.

\section{System Architecture}

\relay{} consists of a perception stage and a control stage connected by one data structure: a mapping from
approach identifier to per-class vehicle count. Everything downstream of that mapping is independent of how the
counts were obtained, which is what allows the same controller to be driven by recorded footage, a live camera,
a synthetic feed, or a microsimulator.

\subsection{Perception}

A single fixed camera views the junction. Each approach is a polygon in normalised image coordinates, drawn once
per site; no per-site model training is performed. For each frame a YOLO detector produces class-labelled boxes,
filtered in four steps. First, per-class confidence gating, because a single threshold is unsuitable for mixed
traffic: two-wheelers are small in the image and are lost at a threshold that suppresses false positives on
cars, so the system uses 0.24 for motorcycles and bicycles, 0.38 by default, 0.50 for trucks and 0.68 for
ambulances. The high ambulance gate is a safety choice: an ambulance detection moves the signal by itself, and a
marginal read on a white van would hand the junction to a non-existent emergency, while a vehicle below the
gate still contributes ordinary demand through its next-best label. Second, cross-class deduplication, since
per-class non-maximum suppression can leave one distant vehicle carrying stacked car, truck and motorcycle
boxes: a greedy pass in descending confidence retains a box only if its intersection over union with every
retained box is below 0.5. Third, approach assignment by centroid containment, followed by an exponential moving
average per approach and class with coefficient 0.35, roughly a one-second time constant at the observed
detection rate. Fourth, failure handling: a bad frame returns the last smoothed counts, and a staleness flag set
after three seconds without an update makes the pipeline feed the controller empty counts, so the wait clocks
and the maximum-wait rule cycle the phases safely instead of holding green on stale demand.

\subsection{Control, topology and deployment}

The controller consumes the count mapping and a set of conflict-free phases, and is topology-agnostic: four-arm,
three-arm and two-arm junctions differ only in the phase set passed at construction, and approaches may be
specified per arm or per movement, in which case the phase set is generated to include paired through phases,
paired protected-turn phases and one split phase per arm. Adding a junction therefore requires drawing polygons
and declaring phases, not changing code. Pedestrian demand enters as a separate transponder-style input giving,
per arm, the number waiting, the longest wait and the number crossing; it is sanitised before use (non-numeric
values, unknown arms, negative counts and non-finite waits are discarded, counts clamped to 200 and waits to
180~s) so a client fault cannot inject an unbounded aging term. The intended installation is one small computer
per junction, taking a feed from an existing camera and driving the existing signal controller, with no central
server in the control path. We measure the perception cost in Section~\ref{sec:perception} but claim no cost
figure, since no hardware was procured.

\section{Control Formulation}
\label{sec:control}

\subsection{Notation and derived quantities}

Let $A$ be the set of approaches and $P$ a set of phases, each $p \in P$ a subset of $A$ served simultaneously
without conflict. Let $\Delta t$ be the control step. At each step perception reports counts $n_{a,c}$ for
approach $a$ and class $c$, and the set of approaches on which an emergency vehicle was detected in the current
frame. Two distinct quantities are derived, the \pcu-weighted demand and the occupancy indicator:
\begin{equation}
d_a = \sum_{c} w_c \, n_{a,c}, \qquad o_a = \mathbb{1}\!\left[\textstyle\sum_c n_{a,c} > 0\right],
\label{eq:demand}
\end{equation}
with weights $w_c$ from Table~\ref{tab:pcu}. Keeping these separate matters: a single motorcycle contributes
$d_a = 0.3$, which a demand threshold would treat as an empty approach, so the empty-phase skip and gap-out
rules key on $o_a$ and a lone two-wheeler still receives a full minimum green.

Each approach carries a clock $r_a$ answering how long the oldest waiting vehicle has waited, not how long since
the approach last had green. With $G$ the set of approaches currently green, $z_a$ the consecutive seconds the
approach has read empty and $\tau_e = 3$~s,
\begin{equation}
r_a \leftarrow
\begin{cases}
r_a \,(1 - \delta_a) & a \in G,\\[2pt]
r_a + \Delta t & a \notin G,\ o_a = 1,\\[2pt]
0 & a \notin G,\ o_a = 0,\ z_a \geq \tau_e,\\[2pt]
r_a & \text{otherwise},
\end{cases}
\label{eq:wait}
\end{equation}
where $\delta_a$ is the fraction of the approach's demand that discharged during the step. On green the clock unwinds by the fraction of the
queue that discharged and reaches zero only when the queue does; resetting it on the green would claim that
everything still behind the stop line had stopped waiting, which beyond capacity is false and lets the red
phase always hold the older clock. The third case is necessary rather than cosmetic: without it a long-vacant
approach accumulates a large clock which the first arriving vehicle inherits, triggering forced service against
a loaded corridor. The debounce keeps a detection dropout from erasing a real queue's guarantee.

\subsection{Phase score}

Define a bounded urgency multiplier $u_a = 1 + \min(r_a, R)/R \in [1,2]$, with $R$ the maximum-wait parameter.
The vehicle pressure of a phase is
\begin{equation}
D(p) = \sum_{a \in p} d_a \, u_a .
\label{eq:vehpressure}
\end{equation}
Aging multiplies each approach's own demand and is capped at a factor of two, so waiting cannot manufacture
demand that does not exist. This is a deliberate departure from an additive term $d_a + \lambda r_a$, which
inverts the intended ordering: with $\lambda = 0.4$ a two-vehicle approach that has waited 45~s scores like an
eighteen-\pcu{} queue and the controller rotates phases at minimum-green cadence. For the same reason the
multiplier is applied per approach rather than at phase level, since a phase-level $\max_a r_a$ lets a starving
single-vehicle turn bay double the score of a phase whose large through queue has just been served.

Pedestrians are treated as demand. A phase $p$ frees the crossing of arm $x$ when neither $x$ nor the opposing
arm, whose straight-ahead movement traverses the same crossing, is served:
$F(p) = \{x : x \notin \mathrm{arms}(p),\ \mathrm{opp}(x) \notin \mathrm{arms}(p)\}$. With $m_x$ people waiting
to cross arm $x$, $k_x$ currently on the crossing and $v_x$ the longest pedestrian wait,
\begin{equation}
\Phi(p) = w_{\mathrm{ped}} \, M(p) \Big( 1 + \frac{\min(\bar{v}(p),\ V)}{V} \Big),
\label{eq:pedpressure}
\end{equation}
where $M(p) = \sum_{x \in F(p)} (m_x + k_x)$ counts the people the phase would serve, $\bar{v}(p)$ is the longest wait among them, $w_{\mathrm{ped}} = 0.3$, so one waiting person contributes about as much pressure as one motorcycle, and
$V$ the pedestrian maximum-wait parameter. The complete score adds a large emergency constant
$B(p) = B_0 \, \mathbb{1}[\exists a \in p : a \in \hat{E}]$ with $B_0 = 10^6$:
\begin{equation}
S(p) = D(p) + \Phi(p) + B(p).
\label{eq:score}
\end{equation}
A detection is a per-frame event and detectors drop frames, so each approach carries a hold timer reset to
$\theta = 2.5$~s whenever an emergency is announced on that arm and otherwise decaying by $\Delta t$; the
latched set is $\hat{E}$. Announcements arrive at arm level and every movement key of that arm is latched,
because the camera cannot resolve which lane the emergency vehicle occupies.

\subsection{Online estimation of arrival and discharge}
\label{sec:estimation}

Both remaining rules need to know how fast an approach fills and how fast it empties. A fixed-time plan obtains
the equivalent from a survey and a saturation-flow table. We estimate both from the count sequence the detector
already produces, which keeps the controller free of per-site constants and lets it follow a junction whose
behaviour changes with the hour and the weather.

Let $d_a(t)$ be the \pcu{} demand on approach $a$ at step $t$. An approach on red cannot discharge, so every
increase in its queue is arrivals; an approach on green moves by arrivals less departures, so departures are
the arrival rate less the observed change:
\begin{align}
\hat{\lambda}_a &\leftarrow \hat{\lambda}_a + \alpha\big(\Delta_a(t) - \hat{\lambda}_a\big),
\label{eq:arrival}\\
\hat{s}_a &\leftarrow \hat{s}_a + \alpha\big(\hat{\lambda}_a - \Delta_a(t) - \hat{s}_a\big),
\label{eq:discharge}
\end{align}
where $\Delta_a(t) = (d_a(t) - d_a(t-1))/\Delta t$, \eqref{eq:arrival} is applied on a step during which the
approach was red, \eqref{eq:discharge} on a step during which it was green with $d_a(t-1) > 0$, $\alpha = \Delta t / \tau$, and both estimates are clamped below at zero. Three details are necessary rather
than cosmetic. The change between two counts was produced under the signal state of the step that has just
ended, so the sample is attributed to that state and not to the one being decided; attributing it to the
current state charges the first green step with a red step's growth and the first red step with a green step's
discharge, and measured 0.49~\pcu/s against a true 0.55. The clamp is applied to the running estimate and never
to the sample, because per-step counts move in whole vehicles and clamping samples discards only the low side
of the noise, inflating $\hat{s}_a$ to 0.79. And~\eqref{eq:discharge} is conditioned on the queue held before the
step, because whether an approach ends a step non-empty depends on whether a vehicle happened to arrive during
it, and conditioning on that deflates the same estimate to 0.30. With all three the estimator recovers 0.51 to
0.60~\pcu/s against a truth of 0.55 across demand levels, and $\hat{\lambda}_a$ to within roughly 20\%. Both
estimates begin at zero, so the two rules below are inert until the junction has been observed for a few
cycles; the controller reduces to a plain hysteresis policy in the interim.

\subsection{Green duration from the measured cycle}
\label{sec:floor}

Webster~\cite{Webster1958TrafficSignalSettings} minimises delay at cycle length $C = (1.5L + 5)/(1 - Y)$, where
$L$ is the lost time per cycle and $Y = \sum_p y_p$ sums the critical flow ratios
$y_p = \max_{a \in p} \lambda_a / s_a$, allocating each phase a green of $(C - L)y_p/Y$. The formula is normally
applied offline to survey data. Equations~\eqref{eq:arrival} and~\eqref{eq:discharge} supply $y_p$ directly, so
the same allocation is evaluated continuously from the junction in front of the camera:
\begin{align}
g^\star(p) &= \max\!\Big(g_{\min},\; (C - L)\frac{y_p}{Y}\Big),
\label{eq:floor}\\
C &= \min\!\Big(C_{\max},\ \frac{1.5L + 5}{1 - Y}\Big),
\label{eq:cycle}
\end{align}
with $C = C_{\max}$ when $Y \geq 1$. $Y$ must sum the critical ratios of one cycle, so phases are accumulated
heaviest-first over disjoint approach sets; on a movement-level junction the phase sets overlap and summing all
of them would invent saturation that is not present. Webster's $C$ diverges as $Y \to 1$ and does not exist
beyond it; the clamp at $C_{\max} = 120$~s, the longest cycle a fixed plan would be given, bounds the floor and
removes the discontinuity. An earlier version of the controller fell back to $g_{\max}$ there, and with a noisy
$Y$ estimate sitting near 1 the floor swung between a green of over 200~s and one of 45~s, the effective cycle
shortened, and the extra clearance time was the whole margin at the highest load; Section~\ref{sec:robustness}
reports the cell where this was found.

$g^\star(p)$ is evaluated once, at the instant the phase takes green, and acts as a floor on that green in place
of $g_{\min}$ in rule 4 of the stage machine. Evaluating it continuously would chase a target that falls as the
queue drains, ending the green for the reason it was extended. It is a floor and not a hold: emergency
preemption and the empty-phase skip are evaluated above it and continue to use $g_{\min}$, so nothing waits
behind a bound intended for a busy junction. Under light demand $Y$ is small, \eqref{eq:floor} evaluates below
$g_{\min}$, and the rule never binds. It is deliberately not capped at $g_{\max}$, which bounds how long one
movement may hold the junction and is a fairness policy; refusing a green the measured demand requires does not
protect the minor approaches, because the queue that was not cleared is still present at the next cycle.

\subsection{Pricing a phase change}
\label{sec:cost}

A phase change costs $y + \rho$ seconds during which no approach discharges. The approaches currently green
would have spent that interval discharging, so the challenger must beat the incumbent by more than what the
handover forfeits:
\begin{equation}
\kappa(p) = \sum_{a \in p} \min\bigl(d_a,\; \hat{s}_a (y + \rho)\bigr),
\label{eq:switchcost}
\end{equation}
and rule 9 of the stage machine switches only when $S(p^\star) > S(p) + h + \kappa(p)$. The minimum caps the
forfeit at what the approach still has queued, so a drained or empty phase costs nothing to leave and the
gap-out and empty-phase rules retain their sensitivity. A constant margin cannot express this: $h$ is the same
whether the junction is empty or saturated, whereas $\kappa$ is zero on an empty junction and grows with the
traffic actually mid-discharge.

\subsection{Target selection and the stage machine}

The signal cycles through green, yellow of duration $y$ and all-red of duration $\rho$; a green is never
followed directly by a conflicting green. The next phase is chosen by strict precedence. \emph{(i) Emergency:}
if $\hat{E} \neq \emptyset$, take the phase serving the most emergency approaches, breaking ties by the largest
$r_a$ among them. \emph{(ii) Vehicle starvation:} otherwise, if any approach has $r_a \geq R$ and $d_a > 0$,
force a phase serving the worst such approach, preferring one that also serves approaches within 25~s of the
threshold so that two clearance intervals are not paid where one would do. Candidates are restricted to
approaches not currently green whose $r_a$ exceeds that of every approach the current phase serves: this rule
serves the worst-treated queue, it does not rotate. Beyond capacity every approach sits past $R$ permanently,
and a rule that fires whenever anyone is past the threshold fires on every step. \emph{(iii) Pedestrian
starvation:} otherwise, if any arm has $m_x > 0$ and $v_x \geq V$, force a phase in $F(x)$ with the greatest
pedestrian demand. \emph{(iv) Score:} otherwise take $\arg\max_p S(p)$ over phases with demand, resting in
all-red if none has.

A running green is terminated by the first applicable rule, where $e$ is elapsed green, $\gamma_t$ the gap-out
time, $\Theta$ the preemption cap, $W$ the walk-hold allowance and $h$ the hysteresis margin:
\begin{enumerate}
\item An emergency exists on another phase and not the current one, and either $e \geq g_{\min}$ or the current
phase is empty.
\item An emergency exists on the current phase: hold, subject to two bounds. An approach reaching $r_a \geq 2R$
forces service even over an active hold, and the cumulative hold is capped at $\Theta$, after which the
emergency term is dropped so the ordinary rules can move the signal.
\item The current phase is empty (no served approach has $o_a = 1$ and no freed crossing has pedestrian demand)
while another phase has demand: switch immediately, since an empty phase has no vehicle to strand.
\item $e < g^\star(p)$: hold, with $g^\star(p) \geq g_{\min}$ allocated at onset by~\eqref{eq:floor}.
\item Vehicle starvation elsewhere, then pedestrian starvation elsewhere: switch.
\item A pedestrian is still on a crossing freed by the current phase and $e < \min(g_{\max}, g_{\min} + W)$: hold.
The bound matters, because an unbounded hold lets a renewing pedestrian stream pin green on a lightly used
approach while a loaded corridor waits.
\item Gap-out: the served approaches have read empty for at least $\gamma_t$ and another phase has demand.
\item Maximum green: $e \geq g_{\max}$, so green is yielded to the best other phase with demand.
\item Score against the cost of switching: $S(p^\star) > S(p) + h + \kappa(p)$ for the best alternative $p^\star$.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Corridor coupling and the preemption bound}

On a corridor each junction $j$ knows, for some arms, the downstream approach $(j', a')$ that the arm's
discharge feeds. The demand presented to the score is discounted by the downstream queue $q_{j',a'}$,
\begin{equation}
\tilde{d}_a =
\begin{cases}
\max\big(d_a - \gamma\, q_{j',a'},\ \varepsilon\big) & o_a = 1,\\
0 & o_a = 0,
\end{cases}
\label{eq:coupling}
\end{equation}
with $\gamma = 0.4$ and $\varepsilon = 0.05$, where the floor at $\varepsilon$ keeps the coupling a discount on pressure rather than an erasure of presence.
This is a one-sided approximation of the pressure difference used in max-pressure theory, where the difference
is taken over turning movements; we use the aggregate downstream queue because it is the only downstream
quantity one camera per junction can supply.

The worst-case delay from the first emergency detection to green on the emergency approach is bounded by the
case in which the announcement arrives immediately after the controller has committed to a transition away from
the very phase that serves the emergency. The committed transition completes, the newly served phase must be
given its minimum green, and only then can the preemption take effect through a second clearance interval:
\begin{equation}
L_{\max} = y + \rho + g_{\min} + y + \rho .
\label{eq:bound}
\end{equation}
With the single-junction parameters of Table~\ref{tab:params}, $L_{\max} = 14.0$~s. The system also ships an
executable self-check asserting twelve properties (clearance, green allocation, fairness, emergency handling
and input validation); all pass on the version evaluated and the captured output accompanies the artefacts.
These are assertions about behaviour under specified stimuli, not proofs, and say nothing about the perception
input.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\subsection{Point-queue simulation and baselines}

Most control results are obtained in a point-queue simulation, a standard abstraction in which delay
accumulates at a single point rather than along a link~\cite{daganzo1997fundamentals}. While an approach
displays green it discharges at $s = 0.55$~veh/s, a saturation headway of about 1.8~s within the range capacity
guidance gives for a through lane~\cite{trb2022hcm}, accumulated as a fractional carry so a green shorter than
one headway releases no vehicle, and with no banking of capacity while the queue is empty. Arrivals are Poisson
per approach, drawn once per step from a single seeded generator and pushed into every policy's queues, so the
comparison is paired at the level of individual vehicles. Runs are 1800~s with the first 300~s discarded, and
vehicles still queued at the end are counted with the wait accumulated so far. The value of this abstraction is
that the controller under test is the system's own code on identical arrival streams; its cost is
traffic-model fidelity, which Experiments 8 and 9 address in SUMO~\cite{lopez2018microscopic}.

Five baselines are used. The \emph{equal-split} plan is a round-robin giving each phase 13.0~s of green, a 35~s
cycle with 3.0~s yellow and 1.5~s all-red; it represents the demand-blind plans the work aims to replace. The
\emph{Webster} plan takes cycle and splits from Webster's formulae computed from the \emph{true} mean demand of
the run: with lost time $L = |P|(y + \rho)$ and critical flow ratios summing to $Y$, the cycle is
$(1.5L + 5)/(1 - Y)$ clamped to $[30, 120]$~s, effective greens $(C - L) y_i / Y$ floored at $g_{\min}$, and when
$Y \geq 0.95$ the cycle is set to the upper clamp with proportional splits. This baseline is deliberately
generous to fixed-time control, being granted perfect knowledge of the average demand and re-derived for every
cell. \emph{Max-pressure} is Varaiya's policy as usually simulated: at every step after the minimum green, take
the phase with the largest queue, with a clearance interval and no other rule. \emph{SOTL} is the
self-organising rule of~\cite{gershenson2005sotl,cools2013sotl}: a red phase accumulates queue times seconds and
takes green when the integral exceeds $\theta = 40$ vehicle-seconds unless the green is discharging fewer than
$\mu = 3$ vehicles. \emph{Actuated} is gap-based vehicle-actuated control~\cite{koonce2008timing}: green is
extended by a 3~s passage time on each arrival up to the maximum green and ends on gap-out, and phases are served
in sequence, skipping phases without a call. All adaptive baselines use the same $g_{\min}$, $g_{\max}$, yellow
and all-red as the controller.

\subsection{Microsimulation}

Experiments 8 and 9 use SUMO \SumoVersion~\cite{lopez2018microscopic} through its TraCI interface. The single
junction has four 300~m arms of two lanes each; vehicles go straight with probability 0.8 and turn left or
right with probability 0.1 each, turning across the opposing stream under permissive right of way. The fleet
is motorcycle-dominated: by count 62\% motorcycles, 30\% cars, 5\% buses and 3\% trucks, with SUMO's class
dynamics and widths narrowed so two-wheelers share a lane, and the sublane model~\cite{erdmann2015lanechange}
enabled at 0.8~m lateral resolution so they filter through gaps. Departures are Poisson per arm at the same rates
as the point-queue profiles. The controller sees only what a camera would: vehicles by class within 80~m of each
stop line, read every 0.5~s; it never reads SUMO's internal queue. Its signal decision is imposed on the SUMO
traffic light each step, so yellow and all-red are the controller's own. The Webster baseline is computed from
the realised \pcu{} demand of each run and a saturation flow of \SumoSat~\pcu/h/lane measured in the same
simulation by holding one approach green behind a standing queue. The actuated baseline is SUMO's own
gap-actuated program with a 3~s maximum gap and the same green bounds. Delay is SUMO's per-vehicle time loss
against free flow, over vehicles departing after a 300~s warm-up in 1800~s runs, \SumoSeeds{} seeds per cell.

\subsection{Experiments}

\textbf{Experiment 1: single junction.} A four-arm junction with two phases under three demand profiles.
\emph{Asymmetric} sets per-approach rates of $(0.150, 0.130, 0.040, 0.035)$~veh/s for N, S, E and W;
\emph{balanced} sets all four to 0.089~veh/s; \emph{single} loads one approach at 0.250~veh/s with 0.035~veh/s on
the others. Each profile is scaled by $m \in \{0.6, 0.8, 1.0, 1.2, 1.4\}$, giving \DemandLow{} to
\DemandHigh~veh/h, with \ExpOneSeeds{} seeds per cell. Per-seed reductions are compared with a paired $t$-test
and a Wilcoxon signed-rank test. A diagnostic (Experiment 1b) measures phase change rate and displayed-green
fraction over 10 seeds per cell.

\textbf{Experiment 2: three-junction corridor, point queues.} A straight arterial carrying a three-arm, a
four-arm and a second three-arm junction at 90~m spacing, with the topology, phase sets, routes and timing of
the system's network demonstration; link travel time is 7.2~s at 12.5~m/s. Four policies: the controller with
coupling~\eqref{eq:coupling}, the same with $\gamma = 0$, an independent Webster plan per junction, and a
coordinated Webster plan with a common cycle and eastbound progression offsets. Runs are 2400~s with 400~s
warm-up, 20 seeds, three demand levels, and one emergency vehicle released from the west at $t = 1200$~s.

\textbf{Experiment 3: preemption latency.} The junction is driven to a loaded state for a random interval of
40 to 160~s, after which an emergency vehicle is announced on a random approach and re-announced every step. We
record the delay until that approach displays green and replay the identical trial against the equal-split
plan, which has no preemption path; 300 trials at each of three demand levels.

\textbf{Experiments 4 and 5: perception.} The shipped detector is evaluated on the system's held-out split, the
full stack is run over 60~s of two real fixed-camera clips at 960~px on an Apple M3 Pro, and the count is
measured as a function of inference resolution with smoothing disabled.

\textbf{Experiment 6: robustness grid.} Seven demand shapes on the four-way junction plus two on a three-arm T,
each at eight load levels running past the point where a fixed plan saturates: \GridCells{} cells of
\GridSeeds{} paired seeds, reported per cell. The grid is repeated with 20\% zero-mean Gaussian miscounting on
the counts the controller sees (Experiment 6a) and, in Experiment 6b, under \BiasModels{} biased count models
matching the detector's actual failure: a saturating undercount $q/(1 + q/q_{1/2})$ with $q_{1/2} \in \{8, 4\}$,
a flat multiplicative loss of 20, 40 and 60\%, and the same losses with a queue of one vehicle rounding to zero
so an occupied approach reads empty, with and without a floor of one.

\textbf{Experiment 7: adaptive baselines.} The \GridCells-cell grid with max-pressure, SOTL and actuated control
added on the same arrivals.

\textbf{Experiment 8: SUMO single junction.} The three Experiment 1 profiles at $m \in \{0.6, 1.0, 1.4\}$,
\SumoSeeds{} seeds, against Webster, max-pressure and SUMO's actuated control.

\textbf{Experiment 9: SUMO corridor.} The Experiment 2 arterial rebuilt in SUMO with \ScorrSpacing~m links, so
a downstream queue can physically block an upstream discharge; coupled, uncoupled, independent Webster and
coordinated Webster, 10 seeds, three levels.

\textbf{Experiment 10: sensitivity.} The fairness bound $R$ from 30~s to unbounded on the single-dominant shape at
the two highest loads, and the motorcycle \pcu{} weight from 0.2 to 1.0 with a class-mixed queue whose
two-wheeler share differs by axis (80\% on N--S, 30\% on E--W), so the weight changes which phase looks heavier.

\subsection{Switching, clearance and what saturation costs}
\label{sec:switching}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{exp1b_switching.pdf}
\caption{Experiment 1b. Left: phase changes per hour. Right: fraction of wall time with a green displayed;
the complement is clearance. Dashed: the equal-split plan, whose behaviour is independent of demand.}
\label{fig:exp1b}
\end{figure}

Balanced saturated demand is where an unconstrained pressure policy fails, and the failure motivates three of
the rules in Section~\ref{sec:control}. No phase dominates, so successive scores sit near-tied and any per-step
comparison flips on noise. A constant hysteresis margin is crossed almost immediately. An anti-starvation rule
that fires whenever any approach has waited past $R$ fires on every step, because beyond capacity every
approach is past $R$ permanently. Both terminate green at $g_{\min}$, and the junction is no longer being
controlled, only alternated. Measured on this junction at 2307~veh/h, a policy with those two properties
displayed 5.2~s of green per 4.5~s of clearance, held green for 0.54 of wall time against the fixed plan's 0.80,
and reached 94~s of mean delay against that plan's 19~s. This is the practical form of the issue Levin et
al.~\cite{Levin2020CyclicalMaxPressure} address with a cyclical structure; Sections~\ref{sec:floor}
and~\ref{sec:cost} recover the lost capacity with a cycle measured rather than configured.

Fig.~\ref{fig:exp1b} shows what the resulting policy does. Under balanced demand \relay{} switches more often
than the fixed plan, \SwitchBalMin{} to \SwitchBalMax{} per hour against \SwitchFixed, and displays green for
less of the wall clock, \GreenFracBalMin{} to \GreenFracBalMax{} against \GreenFracFixed, while reducing delay by
\BalRedWebsterMin\% to \BalRedWebsterMax\%. Displayed green time is therefore not the objective: a fixed plan
spends its green where the demand is not, and slightly less green in the right place beats more of it in the
wrong one. Under a single dominant approach at $m = 1.4$ the effects align and \relay{} switches less than the
fixed plan, \SwitchSinHigh{} per hour, holding green for \GreenFracSinHigh{} of the time.

demand-optimal cycle is over 260~s, the Webster reference is granted its 120~s clamp and is itself unstable, with
a residual queue at the end of the window, and neither controller serves the demand.

This cell is worth dwelling on because an earlier version of the controller lost it by 26.6\%, and the
explanation we first gave was wrong. We attributed the loss to the fairness bound $R$, reasoning that no cycle
short enough to respect a 60~s maximum wait is stable at that load. Experiment 10 then swept $R$ from 30~s to
unbounded and the delay did not move (Section~\ref{sec:sensitivity}). The actual mechanism was in
\eqref{eq:floor}: with the measured $Y$ fluctuating around 1, the earlier fallback to $g_{\max}$ above $Y = 1$
alternated the floor between a green of over 200~s and one of 45~s, the controller ran a shorter effective cycle
than the 120~s plan, and at a degree of saturation of 0.93 the extra 3\% of time spent in clearance was the
entire margin. Clamping the measured cycle at $C_{\max}$, exactly as the fixed plan is clamped, removed the
discontinuity and brought the cell to within a few per cent of the plan with no other cell worse. We report the
sequence because it is the kind of error a per-cell evaluation exists to catch, and because a mean over the grid
would have hidden it.

Repeating the grid with 20\% zero-mean Gaussian miscounting leaves the picture almost unchanged,
\GridNoiseWins{} of \GridCells{} cells won with a worst cell of $\GridNoiseWorstWebster\%$ against Webster,
the one loss being the beyond-capacity cell again. That test is weak, because the estimators smooth
over a $\tau = 60$~s window that averages zero-mean noise away. Experiment 6b applies the error the
detector actually makes (Table~\ref{tab:exp6b}). Under a saturating undercount the controller wins
\BiasSaturatingQEightWins{} and \BiasSaturatingQFourWins{} of \GridCells{} cells with mean reductions of
\BiasSaturatingQEightMeanWebster\% and \BiasSaturatingQFourMeanWebster\%; the single loss in each is the
beyond-capacity cell, where compressing a long queue distorts the saturation estimate the cycle is sized
from. A flat multiplicative loss of 20 or 40\% changes nothing (\BiasScaleZeroEightWins{} and
\BiasScaleZeroSixWins{} cells, means of \BiasScaleZeroEightMeanWebster\% and \BiasScaleZeroSixMeanWebster\%),
because the degree of saturation in~\eqref{eq:floor} is a ratio of arrival and discharge estimates drawn
from the same counts, so a uniform scale cancels, and the phase comparison in~\eqref{eq:score} is invariant
to a common scale. A loss of 60\% collapses to \BiasScaleZeroFourWins{} cells, but not because of the
scale: at that factor a queue of one vehicle rounds to zero, and the models in which a lone vehicle reads
as none fail the same way (\BiasFloorZeroEightWins{} cells) whatever their scale, because the empty-phase
skip and the wait clock are keyed on presence. Giving presence a floor of one restores every one of them
(\BiasScaleZeroFourKeepOneWins, \BiasFloorZeroEightKeepOneWins{} and \BiasFloorZeroSixKeepOneWins{} cells). The
requirement this yields for the perception stack is narrow and testable: the controller needs an
unbiased presence signal at short queues, not accurate absolute counts.

per-class weight partly cancels.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\textbf{This is not a field study.} No signal was controlled. There is no cabinet in the loop, no
communication with a real controller and no before-and-after measurement, so we have no evidence about the
failure modes that appear only in the field: power interruption, lens contamination, camera repositioning,
seasonal light changes, or drivers who learn that the signal responds to them.

\textbf{The fairness bound applies to the visible queue.} Experiment 8 shows that at saturation the vehicles
waiting longest are beyond the detection zone. Any camera-based controller shares this, and it should be
stated to an authority as a condition on camera placement rather than hidden in a mean.

\textbf{Perception was not validated against human ground truth.} The held-out split mixes synthetic frames
with pseudo-labelled real frames, so mAP measures agreement with the labelling process. Experiment 6b bounds
the consequence for control under structured undercounting, but the magnitude of the undercount on the target
city's junctions is unknown and a human-annotated set is required before any absolute demand claim.

\textbf{The microsimulation is calibrated to SUMO defaults, not to a Kathmandu junction.} The fleet shares and
vehicle dynamics are plausible for the region but were not fitted to local measurements, the saturation flow
was measured in the simulator rather than on the road, and the corridor geometry is the demonstration's, not a
surveyed one. The SUMO results establish that the controller's advantage survives car-following, lane
changing, turning conflicts and spillback; they do not establish its magnitude at any particular site.

\textbf{Narrower gaps.} The evaluated junctions have two stages, so movement-level phasing and protected turns
are generated but not exercised. The natural research baseline for a pressure controller is a learned policy,
PressLight~\cite{wei2019presslight} in particular, and that comparison is not made. The pedestrian logic is
asserted by executable invariants but never compared against a conventional pedestrian stage, so we report its
behaviour and not its benefit. The corridor coupling term should be removed or replaced by a movement-level
pressure difference, since neither traffic model supports it.

\section{Conclusion}

We have described a single-camera adaptive signal controller for heterogeneous urban traffic and evaluated it
in a seeded point-queue simulation, in SUMO with a motorcycle-dominated fleet, and on real fixed-camera
footage. The controller converts per-approach, per-class detections into \pcu-weighted demand and selects
phases by a queue-weighted pressure score in the max-pressure family, with clearance intervals, minimum and
maximum green, empty-phase skipping, gap-out, a hard maximum-wait bound for vehicles and a separate one for
pedestrians, and emergency preemption with a bounded hold. Its distinguishing feature is that it measures the
junction rather than being told about it: arrival and discharge rates estimated from the same counts the
detector produces size each green from Webster's cycle on the measured degree of saturation and set the margin
a competing phase must clear.

Against a fixed plan granted the true mean demand the controller reduces mean delay in every cell of the
primary grid and in \GridWins{} of \GridCells{} cells of the robustness grid, the remaining cell being at
parity beyond capacity; it beats plain max-pressure and self-organising control everywhere, and vehicle-actuated
control in \PolActuatedWins{} of \GridCells{} cells with a markedly shorter tail. In SUMO it reduces time loss
in every paired run against Webster, max-pressure and actuated control, by \SumoWebsterMin\% to
\SumoWebsterMax\% against the fixed plan, while its worst-case wait at saturation is longer than the fixed
plans', because the fairness bound reaches only as far as the camera sees. The controller is unaffected by the
biased undercount the detector exhibits as long as an occupied approach never reads empty, and emergency
preemption reaches green within a closed-form bound in \PreTrials{} trials. Two negative results are reported:
the corridor coupling term contributes nothing in either traffic model, and an explanation we first offered for
the one weak cell was wrong and is replaced by a measured one. The next steps follow from the limitations: a
human-annotated perception set from the target city, a comparison against a learned policy on identical
arrivals, and a field trial.
