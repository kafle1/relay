\title{A Self-Calibrating Single-Camera Pressure Controller for Heterogeneous Urban Traffic:
Bounded Fairness, Emergency Preemption and Microsimulation Evaluation}

\begin{abstract}
Fixed-time signal plans remain the default in many South Asian cities, where traffic is motorcycle-dominated,
demand is strongly asymmetric between approaches, and a detector plant is unaffordable. We present \relay, an
adaptive signal controller whose only input is one existing fixed junction camera. A single convolutional
detector yields per-approach, per-class counts, converted to passenger-car-unit (\pcu) demand and consumed by
a queue-weighted pressure controller in the max-pressure family with the operational rules a deployable
signal needs, including a hard anti-starvation bound and emergency preemption with a bounded hold. The
controller estimates each approach's arrival and discharge rate from the counts themselves, with no survey
and no configured saturation flow, and uses them to size each green from Webster's cycle on the measured
degree of saturation and to price a phase change at the discharge its clearance forfeits. It is evaluated
against equal-split and demand-optimal Webster fixed plans, plain max-pressure, self-organising traffic
lights and vehicle-actuated control in a seeded point-queue simulation, and against Webster, max-pressure
and gap-actuated control in SUMO with a motorcycle-dominated fleet. In a point-queue grid of \GridCells{}
cells spanning nine demand shapes on two topologies, mean delay falls against the Webster plan in every cell, by \GridWorstWebster\% in the weakest; in the
separate single-junction experiment the reduction reaches \ExpOneMaxWebster\%; and actuated control is
beaten in \PolActuatedWins{} of the \GridCells{} grid cells. In SUMO the controller reduces
delay in every one of \SumoWebsterRuns{} paired runs against each baseline, by \SumoWebsterMin\% to
\SumoWebsterMax\% against Webster, although at the highest load its worst observed time loss is longer than under either the Webster plan
or actuated control. The controller is unaffected by the biased undercount the
detector actually exhibits, the corridor coupling term contributes at most \ScorrCoupleAbsMax\% of delay in
either traffic model, and emergency preemption reaches green in \PreMeanRelay~s on average over \PreTrials{}
trials with a worst case of \PreWorstRelay~s against a closed-form bound of 14.0~s. This is a simulation and
real-footage study, not a field deployment.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

\IEEEPARstart{U}{rban} signal control in low- and middle-income cities is usually performed by fixed-time
plans or by police officers operating signals by hand, neither of which measures what is queued: a busy
arterial approach holds a long queue while the crossing minor street receives the green it would receive at
three in the morning. Webster's method~\cite{Webster1958TrafficSignalSettings} showed that cycle length and
splits should follow demand, but a fixed plan can only follow the demand measured when it was designed.
Studies of signalised intersections in the Kathmandu valley report saturation flows and delays far from
standard capacity guidance, attributed to the vehicle mixture and lane
discipline~\cite{shrestha2014development,nepali2024assessment}, exactly the condition under which an imported
plan performs badly.

The established remedy is a demand-responsive system such as SCOOT~\cite{Hunt1981SCOOT}
or SCATS~\cite{Sims1980SCATS}, or an optimisation-based controller in the OPAC~\cite{Gartner1983OPAC} and
RHODES~\cite{Mirchandani2001RHODES} lineage. They have decades of field evidence, but they assume a detector plant,
communications and a control room, so per-junction cost is dominated by sensing and civil works; in a city
where the signal head itself is sometimes unpowered, that capital profile is prohibitive.

Two conditions have changed. Fixed cameras are already installed at many urban junctions for enforcement and
monitoring, so a camera-based controller reuses a paid-for asset, and single-stage object detectors now run
at video rate on commodity hardware~\cite{redmon2016yolo,bochkovskiy2020yolov4}, which makes per-approach
counting a software problem. Vision-based queue estimation from fixed cameras is
established~\cite{umair2021efficient}, and several recent studies connect a detector to a signal timer on this
basis~\cite{papanashi2024adaptive,alruyshid2025aibased,talaat2025smart,medvei2025deepsignal}.

What is often missing in that applied literature is the control side: many studies set green in proportion
to a count, rarely state what prevents a minor approach from being starved, what happens when the detector
fails or how the policy behaves at saturation, and compare only against a fixed-time plan, which any
competent adaptive controller will beat. Conversely, the theoretical literature on max-pressure
control~\cite{Varaiya2013MaxPressure} proves throughput optimality under ideal queue observation with no
minimum green, no clearance interval and no fairness bound, all of which a deployable signal requires.

This paper describes and evaluates \relay, a controller placed between those positions. Its contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
\item A single-camera perception-to-control pipeline for heterogeneous traffic in which presence and
\pcu-weighted demand are kept as separate quantities, because a \pcu-weighted score alone classifies a lone
motorcycle as an empty lane.
\item A self-calibrating pressure controller. Arrival and discharge rates are estimated per approach from the
count sequence alone (Section~\ref{sec:estimation}) and used for two decisions a constant cannot make: each
stage's green is floored at its share of Webster's cycle on the measured degree of saturation
(Section~\ref{sec:floor}), and a stage change must beat the discharge its clearance interval forfeits
(Section~\ref{sec:cost}). No saturation-flow table, survey or per-site constant is required.
\item Fairness enforced by explicit bounds rather than by the score: separate maximum-wait bounds for vehicles
and pedestrians, a cap on the preemption hold, and an anti-starvation rule that serves the worst-treated queue
instead of rotating among equally starved ones.
\item An evaluation that reports where the controller loses: against a Webster plan computed from the true
mean demand and three adaptive baselines (plain max-pressure, self-organising traffic lights and
vehicle-actuated control), per cell over a \GridCells-cell grid, and in SUMO with a motorcycle-dominated
fleet, turning movements and finite links; the count error the detector actually makes, a biased
density-dependent undercount, is modelled rather than replaced by zero-mean noise.
\end{itemize}

There is no field installation, no controller cabinet in the loop and
no before-and-after study on a real signal: control results come from seeded simulation and perception results
from recorded footage. The contribution is a design and feasibility study at the level a city could use to
decide whether a pilot is worth funding; Section~\ref{sec:limitations} states which claims a field trial
would still have to establish.

\section{Related Work}

\subsection{Fixed-time, actuated and demand-responsive control}

Webster's analysis of cycle length and green splits~\cite{Webster1958TrafficSignalSettings} remains the
reference method for fixed-time plans and is the basis of the stronger fixed baseline used here.
Vehicle-actuated control, in which a green is extended by a passage time on each detected arrival until a gap
or a maximum is reached, is the most widely deployed adaptive scheme~\cite{koonce2008timing} and is our
strongest adaptive baseline. SCOOT~\cite{Hunt1981SCOOT} adjusts splits, offsets and cycle time from flow
profiles measured upstream of the stop line, SCATS~\cite{Sims1980SCATS} selects among plans using measured
degree of saturation, and OPAC~\cite{Gartner1983OPAC} and RHODES~\cite{Mirchandani2001RHODES} solve a
rolling-horizon optimisation over predicted arrivals. All depend on detectors installed and maintained in the
carriageway, and all were designed for streams with lane discipline and similar vehicle sizes.

\subsection{Max-pressure and self-organising control}

Max-pressure signal control derives from backpressure scheduling in queueing networks~\cite{Tassiulas1992Stability}.
Varaiya~\cite{Varaiya2013MaxPressure} showed that repeatedly activating the phase
maximising a weighted difference between upstream and downstream queues stabilises any demand within the
capacity region using only local queue information. Two properties of the theoretical policy conflict with practice: the argmax may switch as often as the
decision interval allows, and each switch costs a clearance interval during which no approach discharges,
which Levin et al.~\cite{Levin2020CyclicalMaxPressure} address with a cyclical phase structure and our
saturated-demand results quantify independently; and throughput optimality is proved for the unconstrained
policy, so minimum green, maximum green, hysteresis and forced service void the proof. We therefore describe our controller as a queue-weighted pressure heuristic in the
max-pressure family and make no stability claim. Self-organising traffic
lights~\cite{gershenson2005sotl,cools2013sotl} are a second decentralised rule that needs only local counts: a
red phase accumulates waiting vehicles times seconds and takes green when the integral passes a threshold,
unless the current green is discharging a short platoon. It is included as a baseline because it shares our
information requirements and is simpler than our rule.

\subsection{Learning-based control}

Reinforcement learning is the dominant research direction for signal control; surveys by Wei et
al.~\cite{wei2021recent}, Haydari and Yilmaz~\cite{haydari2022deep} and Noaeen et al.~\cite{noaeen2022reinforcement}
cover the field, and PressLight~\cite{wei2019presslight} derives its reward from max-pressure. We did not adopt
a learned policy, for reasons specific to the deployment context: training requires a simulator calibrated to
the target junction and none exists for the junctions we are concerned with; a neural policy does not
naturally expose the maximum-wait or clearance guarantees an authority approving a signal plan needs; and it
transfers poorly across geometries, whereas a pressure rule parameterised only by a phase set moves to a new
junction by drawing polygons on a camera image. The comparison we do not make, and which should be made, is a
head-to-head against a trained policy on the same arrival streams.

\subsection{Camera-based counting and vision-driven signal control}

Vehicle detection from fixed cameras is a mature application of single-stage
detectors~\cite{redmon2016yolo,bochkovskiy2020yolov4}. Our system uses the
Ultralytics implementation of the YOLO family~\cite{jocher2023yolov8}; the architecture generation we
fine-tune is described in~\cite{khanam2024yolov11}, which remains a preprint. Because the controller consumes
instantaneous per-approach occupancy rather than cumulative crossings, we count detections inside approach
polygons instead of tracking vehicles, which removes identity-switch errors in dense queues from the control
path.

Recent applied studies pair such a detector with a signal timer. Papanashi et al.~\cite{papanashi2024adaptive}
report YOLO-based adaptive timing evaluated in Bengaluru, a heterogeneous-traffic context comparable to ours;
Alruyshid et al.~\cite{alruyshid2025aibased} add emergency and bus prioritisation in a Baghdad case study;
Talaat et al.~\cite{talaat2025smart} and Medvei et al.~\cite{medvei2025deepsignal} report detector-driven
controllers, the latter combining detection with a learned policy. These works establish that the perception component is practical; our contribution relative to them is on
the control and evaluation side: an explicit
pressure formulation, stated fairness bounds, a preemption latency bound, and an evaluation against
demand-optimal fixed-time and actuated baselines that reports where the adaptive policy loses.

Emergency preemption has a long operational history and a careful literature on its side effects, since a
preemption interrupts the cycle and can create hazards at the transition~\cite{louisell2004simple}; Humagain et al.~\cite{humagain2020systematic} review route optimisation and preemption methods. Our
detection channel differs: the emergency vehicle is recognised visually by the same detector that counts
ordinary traffic and no vehicle-side equipment is required, which is cheaper and less reliable; that is why the ambulance
class carries the highest confidence threshold and the hold is capped, and what we add is a worst-case
latency bound with a measurement against it.

\section{System Architecture}

\relay{} consists of a perception stage and a control stage connected by one data structure, a mapping from
approach identifier to per-class vehicle count; everything downstream is independent of how the counts were
obtained, so the same controller runs on recorded footage, a live camera, a synthetic feed or a
microsimulator.

\subsection{Perception}

A single fixed camera views the junction. Each approach is a polygon in normalised image coordinates, drawn once
per site; no per-site model training is performed. For each frame a YOLO detector produces class-labelled boxes,
filtered in four steps. First, per-class confidence gating, because two-wheelers are small in the image and are
lost at a threshold that suppresses false positives on cars: 0.24 for motorcycles and bicycles, 0.38 by
default, 0.50 for trucks and 0.68 for ambulances. The high ambulance gate is a safety choice, since an
ambulance detection moves the signal by itself, and a vehicle below the gate still contributes ordinary demand
through its next-best label. Second, cross-class deduplication: a greedy pass in descending confidence retains
a box only if its intersection over union with every retained box is below 0.5, since per-class
non-maximum suppression can leave one distant vehicle carrying stacked car, truck and motorcycle boxes. Third,
approach assignment by centroid containment, followed by an exponential moving average per approach and class
with coefficient 0.35, roughly a one-second time constant at the observed detection rate. Fourth, failure
handling: a bad frame returns the last smoothed counts, and a staleness flag set after three seconds without
an update feeds the controller empty counts, so the wait clocks and the maximum-wait rule cycle the phases
safely instead of holding green on stale demand.

\subsection{Control, topology and deployment}

The controller consumes the count mapping and a set of conflict-free phases, and is topology-agnostic: four-arm,
three-arm and two-arm junctions differ only in the phase set passed at construction, and approaches may be
specified per arm or per movement, in which case the phase set is generated to include paired through phases,
paired protected-turn phases and one split phase per arm. Pedestrian demand enters as a separate input giving,
per arm, the number waiting, the longest wait and the number crossing; it is sanitised before use (unknown
arms, negative counts and non-finite waits are discarded, counts clamped to 200 and waits to 180~s) so a
client fault cannot inject an unbounded aging term. The intended installation is one small computer per
junction, taking a feed from an existing camera and driving the existing signal controller, with no central
server in the control path. We measure the perception cost in Section~\ref{sec:perception} but claim no cost
figure, since no hardware was procured.

\section{Control Formulation}
\label{sec:control}

\subsection{Notation and derived quantities}

Let $A$ be the set of approaches, $P$ a set of phases (each $p \in P$ a subset of $A$ served without
conflict) and $\Delta t$ the control step. Each step perception reports counts $n_{a,c}$ for approach $a$
and class $c$, and the approaches on which an emergency vehicle was detected. Two quantities are derived, the
\pcu-weighted demand and the occupancy indicator:
\begin{equation}
d_a = \sum_{c} w_c \, n_{a,c}, \qquad o_a = \mathbb{1}\!\left[\textstyle\sum_c n_{a,c} > 0\right],
\label{eq:demand}
\end{equation}
with weights $w_c$ of 1.0 for a car, 0.3 for a motorcycle, 2.5 for a bus or truck, 2.0 for an ambulance, 0.2
for a bicycle and 0.8 for an auto-rickshaw: configuration values for a motorcycle-dominated mix, ordered
consistently with published equivalency estimates for South Asian
traffic~\cite{chandra2003effect,arasan2010microsimulation} rather than estimated locally, with their
sensitivity measured in Section~\ref{sec:sensitivity}. Keeping these separate matters: a single motorcycle contributes
$d_a = 0.3$, which a demand threshold would treat as an empty approach, so the empty-phase skip and gap-out
rules key on $o_a$ and a lone two-wheeler still receives a full minimum green.

Each approach carries a clock $r_a$ answering how long the oldest waiting vehicle has waited, not how long since
the approach last had green. With $G$ the set of approaches currently green, $z_a$ the consecutive seconds the
approach has read empty and $\tau_e = 3$~s,
\begin{equation}
r_a \leftarrow
\begin{cases}
r_a \,(1 - \delta_a) & a \in G,\\[2pt]
r_a + \Delta t & a \notin G,\ o_a = 1,\\[2pt]
0 & a \notin G,\ o_a = 0,\ z_a \geq \tau_e,\\[2pt]
r_a & \text{otherwise},
\end{cases}
\label{eq:wait}
\end{equation}
where $\delta_a$ is the fraction of the approach's demand that discharged during the step. On green the clock unwinds by the fraction of the
queue that discharged and reaches zero only when the queue does; resetting it on green would claim that
everything still behind the stop line had stopped waiting, which beyond capacity is false. The third case stops a
long-vacant approach accumulating a clock that the first arriving vehicle inherits, and the debounce keeps a
detection dropout from erasing a real queue's guarantee.

\subsection{Phase score}

Define a bounded urgency multiplier $u_a = 1 + \min(r_a, R)/R \in [1,2]$, with $R$ the maximum-wait parameter.
The vehicle pressure of a phase is
\begin{equation}
D(p) = \sum_{a \in p} d_a \, u_a .
\label{eq:vehpressure}
\end{equation}
Aging multiplies each approach's own demand and is capped at a factor of two, so waiting cannot manufacture
demand that does not exist; an additive term $d_a + \lambda r_a$ inverts the intended ordering (with
$\lambda = 0.4$ a two-vehicle approach that has waited 45~s scores like an eighteen-\pcu{} queue), and a
phase-level $\max_a r_a$ lets a starving single-vehicle turn bay double the score of a phase whose large
through queue has just been served.

Pedestrians are treated as demand. A phase $p$ frees the crossing of arm $x$ when neither $x$ nor the opposing
arm, whose straight-ahead movement traverses the same crossing, is served:
$F(p) = \{x : x \notin \mathrm{arms}(p),\ \mathrm{opp}(x) \notin \mathrm{arms}(p)\}$. With $m_x$ people waiting
to cross arm $x$, $k_x$ currently on the crossing and $v_x$ the longest pedestrian wait,
\begin{equation}
\Phi(p) = w_{\mathrm{ped}} \, M(p) \Big( 1 + \frac{\min(\bar{v}(p),\ V)}{V} \Big),
\label{eq:pedpressure}
\end{equation}
where $M(p) = \sum_{x \in F(p)} (m_x + k_x)$ counts the people the phase would serve, $\bar{v}(p)$ is the longest wait among them, $w_{\mathrm{ped}} = 0.3$, so one waiting person contributes about as much pressure as one motorcycle, and
$V$ the pedestrian maximum-wait parameter. The complete score adds a large emergency constant
$B(p) = B_0 \, \mathbb{1}[\exists a \in p : a \in \hat{E}]$ with $B_0 = 10^6$:
\begin{equation}
S(p) = D(p) + \Phi(p) + B(p).
\label{eq:score}
\end{equation}
Detectors drop frames, so each approach carries a hold timer reset to $\theta = 2.5$~s whenever an emergency
is announced on that arm and otherwise decaying by $\Delta t$; the latched set is $\hat{E}$, and every
movement of an arm is latched together because the camera cannot resolve the lane.

\subsection{Online estimation of arrival and discharge}
\label{sec:estimation}

Both remaining rules need to know how fast an approach fills and how fast it empties, which a fixed-time plan
takes from a survey and a saturation-flow table. We estimate both from the count sequence the detector
already produces, which keeps the controller free of per-site constants.

Let $d_a(t)$ be the \pcu{} demand on approach $a$ at step $t$. An approach on red cannot discharge, so every
increase in its queue is arrivals; an approach on green moves by arrivals less departures, so departures are
the arrival rate less the observed change:
\begin{align}
\hat{\lambda}_a &\leftarrow \hat{\lambda}_a + \alpha\big(\Delta_a(t) - \hat{\lambda}_a\big),
\label{eq:arrival}\\
\hat{s}_a &\leftarrow \hat{s}_a + \alpha\big(\hat{\lambda}_a - \Delta_a(t) - \hat{s}_a\big),
\label{eq:discharge}
\end{align}
where $\Delta_a(t) = (d_a(t) - d_a(t-1))/\Delta t$, \eqref{eq:arrival} is applied on a step during which the
approach was red, \eqref{eq:discharge} on a step during which it was green with $d_a(t-1) > 0$, $\alpha = \Delta t / \tau$, and both estimates are clamped below at zero. Three details matter. The change between two counts was produced under the signal state of the step that has
just ended, so the sample is attributed to that state and not to the one being decided; attributing it to the
current state charges the first green step with a red step's growth and measured 0.49~\pcu/s against a true
0.55. The clamp is applied to the running estimate and never to the sample, because clamping samples discards
only the low side of the noise and inflates $\hat{s}_a$ to 0.79. And~\eqref{eq:discharge} is conditioned on
the queue held before the step, not after it, since conditioning on the latter deflates the estimate to 0.30.
With all three the estimator recovers 0.51 to 0.60~\pcu/s against a truth of 0.55, and $\hat{\lambda}_a$
to within roughly 20\%; both start at zero, so the two rules below are inert for the first few cycles.

\subsection{Green duration from the measured cycle}
\label{sec:floor}

Webster~\cite{Webster1958TrafficSignalSettings} minimises delay at cycle length $C = (1.5L + 5)/(1 - Y)$, where
$L$ is the lost time per cycle and $Y = \sum_p y_p$ sums the critical flow ratios
$y_p = \max_{a \in p} \lambda_a / s_a$, allocating each phase a green of $(C - L)y_p/Y$. Equations~\eqref{eq:arrival} and~\eqref{eq:discharge} supply $y_p$ directly, so the allocation normally
applied offline to survey data is evaluated continuously from the junction in front of the camera:
\begin{align}
g^\star(p) &= \max\!\Big(g_{\min},\; (C - L)\frac{y_p}{Y}\Big),
\label{eq:floor}\\
C &= \min\!\Big(C_{\max},\ \frac{1.5L + 5}{1 - Y}\Big),
\label{eq:cycle}
\end{align}
with $C = C_{\max}$ when $Y \geq 1$. $Y$ must sum the critical ratios of one cycle, so phases are accumulated heaviest-first over disjoint
approach sets, since on a movement-level junction the phase sets overlap. Webster's $C$ diverges as $Y \to 1$ and does not exist
beyond it; the clamp at $C_{\max} = 120$~s, the longest cycle a fixed plan would be given, bounds the floor and
removes the discontinuity. An earlier version fell back to $g_{\max}$ there, and with a noisy $Y$ near 1 the floor swung between
greens of over 200~s and 45~s; Section~\ref{sec:robustness} reports the cell where this was found.

$g^\star(p)$ is evaluated once, when the phase takes green, and acts as a floor on that green in place of
$g_{\min}$ in rule 4 of the stage machine; evaluating it continuously would chase a target that falls as the
queue drains. It is a floor and not a hold: preemption and the empty-phase skip are evaluated above it and
still use $g_{\min}$. Under light demand $Y$ is small and the rule never binds. It is deliberately not capped
at $g_{\max}$, which is a fairness bound on how long one movement may hold the junction; refusing a green the
measured demand requires does not protect the minor approaches, because the uncleared queue is still there
at the next cycle.

\subsection{Pricing a phase change}
\label{sec:cost}

A phase change costs $y + \rho$ seconds during which no approach discharges. The approaches currently green
would have spent that interval discharging, so the challenger must beat the incumbent by more than what the
handover forfeits:
\begin{equation}
\kappa(p) = \sum_{a \in p} \min\bigl(d_a,\; \hat{s}_a (y + \rho)\bigr),
\label{eq:switchcost}
\end{equation}
and rule 9 of the stage machine switches only when $S(p^\star) > S(p) + h + \kappa(p)$. The minimum caps the forfeit at what the approach still has queued, so a drained or empty phase costs
nothing to leave; a constant margin $h$ cannot express this, being the same whether the junction is empty or
saturated.

\subsection{Target selection and the stage machine}

The signal cycles through green, yellow of duration $y$ and all-red of duration $\rho$; a green is never
followed directly by a conflicting green. The next phase is chosen by strict precedence. \emph{(i) Emergency:}
if $\hat{E} \neq \emptyset$, take the phase serving the most emergency approaches, breaking ties by the largest
$r_a$ among them. \emph{(ii) Vehicle starvation:} otherwise, if any approach has $r_a \geq R$ and $d_a > 0$,
force a phase serving the worst such approach, preferring one that also serves approaches within 25~s of the
threshold so that two clearance intervals are not paid where one would do. Candidates are restricted to approaches not currently green whose $r_a$ exceeds that of every approach the
current phase serves: the rule serves the worst-treated queue and does not rotate, since beyond capacity
every approach sits past $R$ permanently. \emph{(iii) Pedestrian
starvation:} otherwise, if any arm has $m_x > 0$ and $v_x \geq V$, force a phase in $F(x)$ with the greatest
pedestrian demand. \emph{(iv) Score:} otherwise take $\arg\max_p S(p)$ over phases with demand, resting in
all-red if none has.

A running green is terminated by the first applicable rule, where $e$ is elapsed green, $\gamma_t$ the gap-out
time, $\Theta$ the preemption cap, $W$ the walk-hold allowance and $h$ the hysteresis margin:
\begin{enumerate}
\item An emergency exists on another phase and not the current one, and either $e \geq g_{\min}$ or the current
phase is empty.
\item An emergency exists on the current phase: hold, except that an approach reaching $r_a \geq 2R$ forces
service and the cumulative hold is capped at $\Theta$, after which the emergency term is dropped.
\item The current phase is empty (no served approach has $o_a = 1$, no freed crossing has pedestrian demand)
while another phase has demand: switch immediately.
\item $e < g^\star(p)$: hold, with $g^\star(p) \geq g_{\min}$ allocated at onset by~\eqref{eq:floor}.
\item Vehicle starvation elsewhere, then pedestrian starvation elsewhere: switch.
\item A pedestrian is still on a crossing freed by the current phase and $e < \min(g_{\max}, g_{\min} + W)$:
hold; the bound stops a renewing pedestrian stream pinning green on a lightly used approach.
\item Gap-out: the served approaches have read empty for at least $\gamma_t$ and another phase has demand.
\item Maximum green: $e \geq g_{\max}$: yield to the best other phase with demand.
\item Score against the cost of switching: $S(p^\star) > S(p) + h + \kappa(p)$ for the best alternative $p^\star$.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Corridor coupling and the preemption bound}

On a corridor each junction $j$ knows, for some arms, the downstream approach $(j', a')$ that the arm's
discharge feeds. The demand presented to the score is discounted by the downstream queue $q_{j',a'}$,
\begin{equation}
\tilde{d}_a =
\begin{cases}
\max\big(d_a - \gamma\, q_{j',a'},\ \varepsilon\big) & o_a = 1,\\
0 & o_a = 0,
\end{cases}
\label{eq:coupling}
\end{equation}
with $\gamma = 0.4$ and $\varepsilon = 0.05$, where the floor at $\varepsilon$ keeps the coupling a discount on pressure rather than an erasure of presence.
This is a one-sided approximation of the movement-level pressure difference of max-pressure theory, using
the aggregate downstream queue because it is the only downstream quantity one camera per junction can supply.

The worst case from first emergency detection to green is an announcement that arrives just after the
controller has committed to a transition away from the phase that serves the emergency: the transition
completes, the new phase must be given its minimum green, and only then can the preemption take effect
through a second clearance interval:
\begin{equation}
L_{\max} = y + \rho + g_{\min} + y + \rho .
\label{eq:bound}
\end{equation}
With the single-junction parameters (supplementary Table~S1), $L_{\max} = 14.0$~s. The system also ships an executable self-check asserting twelve properties (clearance, green allocation,
fairness, emergency handling and input validation), all passing on the version evaluated; these are
assertions under specified stimuli, not proofs.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\subsection{Point-queue simulation and baselines}

Most control results are obtained in a point-queue simulation, in which delay accumulates at a single point
rather than along a link. While an approach displays green it discharges at
$s = 0.55$~veh/s, a saturation headway of about 1.8~s within the range capacity guidance gives for a through
lane~\cite{trb2022hcm}, accumulated as a fractional carry so a green shorter than one headway releases no
vehicle, with no banking of capacity while the queue is empty. Arrivals are Poisson per approach, drawn once
per step from a single seeded generator and pushed into every policy's queues, so the comparison is paired at
the level of individual vehicles. Runs are 1800~s with the first 300~s discarded, and vehicles still queued at
the end are counted with the wait accumulated so far. The controller under test is the system's own code on
identical arrival streams; the cost is traffic-model fidelity, which Experiments 8 and 9 address in
SUMO~\cite{lopez2018microscopic}.

Five baselines are used. The \emph{equal-split} plan is a round-robin giving each phase 13.0~s of green, a
35~s cycle with 3.0~s yellow and 1.5~s all-red.
The \emph{Webster} plan takes cycle and splits from Webster's formulae computed from the \emph{true} mean
demand of the run: with lost time $L = |P|(y + \rho)$ and critical flow ratios summing to $Y$, the cycle is
$(1.5L + 5)/(1 - Y)$ clamped to $[30, 120]$~s, effective greens $(C - L) y_i / Y$ floored at $g_{\min}$, and
when $Y \geq 0.95$ the cycle is set to the upper clamp with proportional splits; it is deliberately generous
to fixed-time control, being granted perfect knowledge of the average demand and re-derived for every cell.
\emph{Max-pressure} is Varaiya's policy as usually simulated: at every step after the minimum green, take the
phase with the largest queue, with a clearance interval and no other rule. \emph{SOTL} is the self-organising
rule of~\cite{gershenson2005sotl,cools2013sotl} with threshold $\theta = 40$ vehicle-seconds and platoon
cut-off $\mu = 3$. \emph{Actuated} is gap-based vehicle-actuated control~\cite{koonce2008timing}: green is
extended by a 3~s passage time on each arrival up to the maximum green and ends on gap-out, and phases are
served in sequence, skipping phases without a call. All adaptive baselines use the same $g_{\min}$,
$g_{\max}$, yellow and all-red as the controller.

\subsection{Microsimulation}

Experiments 8 and 9 use SUMO \SumoVersion~\cite{lopez2018microscopic} through its TraCI interface. The single
junction has four 300~m arms of two lanes each; vehicles go straight with probability 0.8 and turn left or
right with probability 0.1 each, across the opposing stream under permissive right of way. The fleet is
62\% motorcycles, 30\% cars, 5\% buses and 3\% trucks by count, with widths narrowed so two-wheelers share a
lane and the sublane model~\cite{erdmann2015lanechange} at 0.8~m lateral resolution so they filter through
gaps; departures are Poisson per arm at the point-queue rates. The controller sees only what a camera would,
vehicles by class within 80~m of each stop line read every 0.5~s, never SUMO's internal queue, and its
decision is imposed on the SUMO traffic light each step. The Webster baseline is computed from the realised
\pcu{} demand of each run and a saturation flow of \SumoSat~\pcu/h/lane measured in the same simulation
behind a standing queue; the actuated baseline is SUMO's own gap-actuated program with a 3~s maximum gap and
the same green bounds. Delay is SUMO's per-vehicle time loss against free flow over vehicles departing after
a 300~s warm-up in 1800~s runs, \SumoSeeds{} seeds per cell.

\subsection{Experiments}

\textbf{Experiment 1: single junction.} A four-arm junction with two phases under three demand profiles:
\emph{asymmetric}, $(0.150, 0.130, 0.040, 0.035)$~veh/s for N, S, E and W; \emph{balanced}, 0.089~veh/s on all
four; \emph{single}, 0.250~veh/s on one approach and 0.035~veh/s on the others; each scaled by
$m \in \{0.6, 0.8, 1.0, 1.2, 1.4\}$, giving \DemandLow{} to \DemandHigh~veh/h, \ExpOneSeeds{} seeds per cell,
with paired $t$ and Wilcoxon signed-rank tests on the per-seed reductions. Experiment 1b measures phase change
rate and displayed-green fraction over 10 seeds per cell.

\textbf{Experiment 2: three-junction corridor, point queues.} A straight arterial carrying a three-arm, a
four-arm and a second three-arm junction at 90~m spacing, with the topology, phase sets, routes and timing of
the system's network demonstration; link travel time 7.2~s at 12.5~m/s. Four policies: the controller with
coupling~\eqref{eq:coupling}, the same with $\gamma = 0$, an independent Webster plan per junction, and a
coordinated Webster plan with a common cycle and eastbound progression offsets. Runs are 2400~s with 400~s
warm-up, 20 seeds, three demand levels, and one emergency vehicle released from the west at $t = 1200$~s.

\textbf{Experiment 3: preemption latency.} The junction is driven to a loaded state for a random 40 to
160~s, then an emergency vehicle is announced on a random approach and re-announced every step; the delay
until that approach displays green is recorded and the identical trial replayed against the equal-split
plan, which has no preemption path; 300 trials at each of three demand levels.

\textbf{Experiment 4: detector.} The shipped detector on the system's held-out split.
\textbf{Experiment 5: footage.} The full stack over 60~s of two real fixed-camera clips at 960~px on an
Apple M3 Pro, and the count as a function of inference resolution with smoothing disabled.

\textbf{Experiment 6: robustness grid.} Seven demand shapes on the four-way junction plus two on a three-arm
T, each at eight load levels running past the point where a fixed plan saturates: \GridCells{} cells of
\GridSeeds{} paired seeds, reported per cell; repeated with 20\% zero-mean Gaussian miscounting (6a) and under
\BiasModels{} biased count models matching the detector's actual failure (6b): a saturating undercount
$q/(1 + q/q_{1/2})$ with $q_{1/2} \in \{8, 4\}$, flat losses of 20, 40 and 60\%, and the same losses with a
queue of one vehicle rounding to zero, with and without a floor of one.

\textbf{Experiment 7: adaptive baselines.} The grid with max-pressure, SOTL and actuated control on the same
arrivals.

\textbf{Experiment 8: SUMO single junction.} The three Experiment 1 profiles at $m \in \{0.6, 1.0, 1.4\}$,
\SumoSeeds{} seeds, against Webster, max-pressure and SUMO's actuated control.

\textbf{Experiment 9: SUMO corridor.} The Experiment 2 arterial rebuilt in SUMO with \ScorrSpacing~m links,
so a downstream queue can physically block an upstream discharge; the four policies of Experiment 2, 10
seeds, three levels.

\textbf{Experiment 10: sensitivity.} The fairness bound $R$ from 30~s to unbounded on the single-dominant shape
at the two highest loads, and the motorcycle \pcu{} weight from 0.2 to 1.0 with a class-mixed queue whose
two-wheeler share differs by axis (80\% on N--S, 30\% on E--W).

\subsection{Single junction}
\label{sec:single}

Fig.~\ref{fig:exp1} gives the single-junction results, with per-cell values in supplementary Table~S2.
\relay{} reduces mean delay against both baselines in all \ExpOneCells{} cells, by \ExpOneMinWebster\% to
\ExpOneMaxWebster\% against the Webster plan and by \ExpOneMinEqual\% to \ExpOneMaxEqual\% against the
equal-split plan, every difference significant at $p < 0.05$ by the Wilcoxon signed-rank test (largest
$p = \ExpOneWorstP$). Under asymmetric demand, the regime the system is designed for, the reduction is
\AsyMidRedEqual\% against equal split and \AsyMidRedWebster\% against Webster at \AsyMidDemand~veh/h. The gain
against equal split grows with demand, reaching \AsyHighRedEqual\% at \AsyHighDemand~veh/h, but that growth
is a capacity artefact: there the equal-split plan is oversaturated on the major approaches and its delay
diverges, whereas the Webster plan and \relay{} remain stable at essentially the same throughput,
\AsyHighThrWebster{} and \AsyHighThrRelay~veh/h. The gain against Webster is close to flat across the range,
\AsyLowRedWebster\% down to \AsyHighRedWebster\%, and we regard the Webster column as the honest headline.
Under a single dominant approach the equal-split plan collapses (at \SinMidDemand~veh/h a mean delay of
\SinMidDelayEqual~s and a throughput of \SinMidThrEqual~veh/h against the demand offered); against Webster the
gain peaks at \SinBestRedWebster\% at \SinBestDemand~veh/h and is \SinHighRedWebster\% at the highest load.
Under balanced demand, the hard case for a pressure policy, the gain against Webster is \BalLowRedWebster\%
at \BalLowDemand~veh/h and \BalHighRedWebster\% at \BalHighDemand~veh/h.

The wait distribution (supplementary Fig.~S1) is where the fairness bound becomes visible. At
\AsyMidDemand~veh/h under asymmetric demand the 95th-percentile wait is \AsyMidPNinetyFiveEqual~s under equal
split, \AsyMidPNinetyFiveWebster~s under Webster and \AsyMidPNinetyFiveRelay~s under \relay, with worst
observed waits of \AsyMidMaxEqual, \AsyMidMaxWebster{} and \AsyMidMaxRelay~s. Under a single dominant approach
the contrast is larger: \SinMidPNinetyFiveEqual{} and \SinMidPNinetyFiveWebster~s at the 95th percentile
against \SinMidPNinetyFiveRelay~s, and worst waits of \SinMidMaxEqual{} and \SinMidMaxWebster~s against
\SinMidMaxRelay~s, consistent with the forced-service rule at $R = 60$~s. The tail improves in every cell,
which matters more than the mean for a policy whose fairness claim is a bound.

\subsection{Switching, clearance and what saturation costs}
\label{sec:switching}

Balanced saturated demand is where an unconstrained pressure policy fails, and the failure motivates three of
the rules in Section~\ref{sec:control}. No phase dominates, successive scores sit near-tied, a constant
hysteresis margin is crossed on noise, and an anti-starvation rule that fires whenever any approach has
waited past $R$ fires on every step, because beyond capacity every approach is past $R$ permanently. Both
terminate green at $g_{\min}$: measured on this junction at 2307~veh/h, a policy with those two properties
displayed 5.2~s of green per 4.5~s of clearance, held green for 0.54 of wall time against the fixed plan's
0.80, and reached 94~s of mean delay against that plan's 19~s, the practical form of the issue Levin et
al.~\cite{Levin2020CyclicalMaxPressure} address with a cyclical structure. Sections~\ref{sec:floor}
and~\ref{sec:cost} recover the lost capacity with a cycle measured rather than configured: under balanced
demand \relay{} still switches more often than the fixed plan (Experiment 1b, supplementary Fig.~S2),
\SwitchBalMin{} to \SwitchBalMax{} per hour against \SwitchFixed, and displays green for less of the wall
clock, \GreenFracBalMin{} to \GreenFracBalMax{} against \GreenFracFixed, while reducing delay by
\BalRedWebsterMin\% to \BalRedWebsterMax\%, because a fixed plan spends its green where the demand is not.
Under a single dominant approach at $m = 1.4$ \relay{} switches less than the fixed plan, \SwitchSinHigh{}
per hour, holding green for \GreenFracSinHigh{} of the time.

\subsection{Corridor, point queues}

Experiment 2 (supplementary Table~S3) gives three findings, one negative. The delay reduction decays only
gradually with demand: \CorrRedWebsterLow\%, \CorrRedWebsterMid\% and \CorrRedWebsterHigh\% against
independent Webster plans at \CorrDemandLow, \CorrDemandMid{} and \CorrDemandHigh~veh/h, and
\CorrRedCoordLow\%, \CorrRedCoordMid\% and \CorrRedCoordHigh\% against the coordinated plan, with through
travel time and stops improving together with delay (\CorrTravelRelayHigh~s and \CorrStopsRelayHigh{} stops
against the coordinated plan's \CorrTravelCoordHigh~s and \CorrStopsCoordHigh{} at the highest demand). The
coupling term does essentially nothing, changing mean delay by \CorrRedIsolatedLow\%, \CorrRedIsolatedMid\%
and \CorrRedIsolatedHigh\% when disabled, because a point queue has no spatial extent and a downstream queue
never physically blocks an upstream discharge; Experiment 9 asks the same question with links that can fill.
The emergency vehicle traverses the corridor in \CorrAmbRelayMin{} to \CorrAmbRelayMax~s under \relay{}
against a free-flow 21.6~s and \CorrAmbWebsterMin{} to \CorrAmbWebsterMax~s under independent Webster plans;
the coordinated plan manages \CorrAmbCoordMin{} to \CorrAmbCoordMax~s because the vehicle travels in the
progression direction, whereas \relay{} is direction-agnostic.

\subsection{Emergency preemption latency}

Mean latency is \PreMeanRelay~s against \PreMeanFixed~s for the fixed cycle over \PreTrials{} trials
(supplementary Table~S4), almost insensitive to demand (\PreLightMean~s at the lightest load,
\PreHeavyMean~s at the heaviest) because preemption is evaluated above the measured-cycle floor against
$g_{\min}$, so a busier junction does not make an emergency vehicle wait longer. The tail is the important
quantity: the 95th percentile is \PreAllRelayPNinetyFive~s against \PreAllFixedPNinetyFive~s and the worst
case \PreWorstRelay~s against \PreWorstFixed~s, just below the bound of 14.0~s from~\eqref{eq:bound}; every
trial above 13.4~s is one in which the announcement arrived within the first second of a yellow already
transitioning away from the emergency's own phase. Because the bound is composed only of clearance and
minimum-green settings, it can be stated in advance to an emergency service. Two qualifications: in
\PreAlreadyGreenPct\% of trials the emergency approach already had green when the vehicle was detected, and
in \PreFixedSoonerPct\% the fixed cycle happened to reach that approach sooner than the preemption did, so
the distributions overlap (supplementary Fig.~S3) even though the tails differ sharply. The claim these data
support is a bounded worst case, not a uniformly faster response.

\subsection{Perception}
\label{sec:perception}

On the held-out split of \DetValImages{} images and \DetValInstances{} labelled instances, the
\DetParams-million-parameter detector reaches a mean average precision of \DetMapFifty{} at IoU 0.5 and
\DetMapFiftyNinetyFive{} averaged over IoU 0.5 to 0.95, with precision \DetPrecision{} and recall
\DetRecall{}; per-class AP at IoU 0.5 (supplementary Fig.~S4) exceeds
0.96 for cars, buses, trucks and ambulances and is lowest for motorcycles at \DetApMotorcycle, the smallest
and most occluded class. The split mixes automatically labelled frames from a synthetic feed with real frames
pseudo-labelled by a general detector, so a high score establishes that the fine-tuned model reproduces the
labelling function it was trained against, not accuracy against human ground truth.

On real footage at 960~px, median per-frame latency is \LatLevMedian~ms on the boulevard clip (\LatLevFps{}
frames per second, 95th percentile \LatLevPNinetyFive~ms) and \LatHcmcMedian~ms on the dense mixed-traffic
clip (\LatHcmcFps{} frames per second), well above what a control loop with a 5~s minimum green needs.

The main perception weakness is visible on the dense clip (supplementary Fig.~S5): a few dozen vehicles are
detected in a scene containing several hundred. Against inference resolution (supplementary Fig.~S4) the
mean sampled count on that clip rises from \ResHcmcSixFourZero{} at 640~px to \ResHcmcNineSixZero{} at
960~px, \ResHcmcOneTwoEightZero{} at 1280~px and \ResHcmcOneSixZeroZero{} at 1600~px, an increase of
\ResHcmcRisePct\% with no sign of convergence (motorcycles alone \ResHcmcMotoRisePct\%), on the sparser
boulevard clip from \ResLevSixFourZero{} to \ResLevOneSixZeroZero, at a median inference cost rising from
\ResHcmcMsSixFourZero{} to \ResHcmcMsOneSixZeroZero~ms. A count that keeps growing with resolution has not
converged, so the absolute demand the controller receives on dense two-wheeler scenes is an underestimate of
unknown magnitude, worse where the queue is denser. This does not immediately invalidate the control
results, because the controller compares approaches rather than reading absolute volumes; Experiment 6b
measures how much it matters.

Table~\ref{tab:exp6} reports the wider grid. \relay{} reduces mean delay against both fixed-time baselines in
\GridWins{} of \GridCells{} cells spanning \GridDemandLow{} to \GridDemandHigh~veh/h, and never falls below

delay against \GridWorstCellWebsterDelay~s, at throughputs of \GridWorstCellRelayThr{} and
\GridWorstCellWebsterThr~veh/h. There the dominant approach alone demands 82\% of all displayed green, the demand-optimal cycle is over
260~s, and neither controller serves the demand.

This cell is worth dwelling on because an earlier version of the controller lost it by 26.6\%, and the
explanation we first gave, the fairness bound $R$, was wrong: Experiment 10 swept $R$ from 30~s to unbounded
and the delay did not move (Section~\ref{sec:sensitivity}). The mechanism was in \eqref{eq:floor}: with the
measured $Y$ fluctuating around 1, the earlier fallback to $g_{\max}$ above $Y = 1$ alternated the floor
between a green of over 200~s and one of 45~s, the controller ran a shorter effective cycle than the 120~s
plan, and at a degree of saturation of 0.93 the extra 3\% of time in clearance was the entire margin.
Clamping the measured cycle at $C_{\max}$, as the fixed plan is clamped, brought the cell to within a few per
cent of the plan with no other cell worse; we report the sequence because it is the kind of error a per-cell
evaluation exists to catch.

Repeating the grid with 20\% zero-mean Gaussian miscounting leaves the picture almost unchanged,
\GridNoiseWins{} of \GridCells{} cells won with a worst of $\GridNoiseWorstWebster\%$ against Webster, the
one loss the beyond-capacity cell again; that test is weak, because the estimators smooth over a
$\tau = 60$~s window that averages zero-mean noise away. Experiment 6b applies the error the detector
actually makes (supplementary Table~S5). Under a saturating undercount the controller wins
\BiasSaturatingQEightWins{} and \BiasSaturatingQFourWins{} of \GridCells{} cells with mean reductions of
\BiasSaturatingQEightMeanWebster\% and \BiasSaturatingQFourMeanWebster\%, the single loss in each being the
beyond-capacity cell, where compressing a long queue distorts the saturation estimate. A flat loss of 20 or
40\% changes nothing (\BiasScaleZeroEightWins{} and \BiasScaleZeroSixWins{} cells), because the degree of
saturation in~\eqref{eq:floor} is a ratio of estimates drawn from the same counts and the phase comparison
in~\eqref{eq:score} is invariant to a common scale. A loss of 60\% collapses to \BiasScaleZeroFourWins{}
cells, but not because of the scale: at that factor a queue of one vehicle rounds to zero, and the models in
which a lone vehicle reads as none fail the same way (\BiasFloorZeroEightWins{} cells) whatever their scale,
because the empty-phase skip and the wait clock are keyed on presence. A presence floor of one restores
every one of them (\BiasScaleZeroFourKeepOneWins, \BiasFloorZeroEightKeepOneWins{} and
\BiasFloorZeroSixKeepOneWins{} cells). The requirement this yields for the perception stack is narrow and
testable: an unbiased presence signal at short queues, not accurate absolute counts.

\subsection{Adaptive baselines}
\label{sec:policies}

Experiment 7 (supplementary Fig.~S6) adds the adaptive baselines on the same grid. Against plain max-pressure
\relay{} wins \PolMaxpressureWins{} of \GridCells{} cells with a worst of $\PolMaxpressureWorst\%$ and a
median of \PolMaxpressureMedian\%; against SOTL \PolSotlWins{} cells, worst $\PolSotlWorst\%$, median
\PolSotlMedian\%. Vehicle-actuated control is the serious competitor: \relay{} wins \PolActuatedWins{} of
\GridCells{} cells with a median reduction of \PolActuatedMedian\% and loses \PolActuatedLosses{}, all at or
beyond capacity on balanced or near-balanced shapes, by at most \PolActuatedLossMax\%. The mean advantage is
modest; the difference lies in the tails. Averaged over the grid, the 95th-percentile wait is
\PolRelayPNinetyFive~s under \relay{} against \PolActuatedPNinetyFive~s under actuated control,
\PolMaxpressurePNinetyFive~s under max-pressure and \PolSotlPNinetyFive~s under SOTL, and the worst wait
\PolRelayMax~s against \PolActuatedMax, \PolMaxpressureMax{} and \PolSotlMax~s. Max-pressure and SOTL have no fairness bound and their tails show it; actuated control's sequential
service gives it one, so the honest summary is that \relay{} is an actuated controller that needs no
detectors in the road, sizes its greens from a measured cycle, and carries preemption.

\subsection{Microsimulation}
\label{sec:sumo}

Fig.~\ref{fig:exp8} gives the microsimulation result, with per-cell values in supplementary Table~S6. \relay{} reduces mean time loss
in every one of the \SumoWebsterRuns{} paired runs against each of the three baselines: by \SumoWebsterMin\% to
\SumoWebsterMax\% against the Webster plan, \SumoMaxpressureMin\% to \SumoMaxpressureMax\% against max-pressure,
and \SumoActuatedMin\% to \SumoActuatedMax\% against SUMO's actuated program. The margin over actuated control is larger than in the point-queue harness, and the reason is visible in the traffic: with a
motorcycle-dominated fleet a gap-actuated detector sees a continuous stream of small headways and extends
green to its maximum on the busy approach, whereas the measured-cycle floor sizes the green to the degree of
saturation and hands over sooner. The reduction falls with load in every profile, as it does in the point-queue grid.

One result goes the other way. At the highest load the worst observed time loss is
\SumoHighMaxRelay~s under \relay{} against \SumoHighMaxWebster~s under Webster and \SumoHighMaxActuated~s under
actuated control, averaged over the three profiles, and the 95th percentile is \SumoHighPNinetyFiveRelay~s
against \SumoHighPNinetyFiveWebster~s. In the point-queue harness the fairness bound held the tail; in SUMO it
does not, because the bound applies to what the camera sees. A vehicle beyond the 80~m detection zone is on no wait clock, and under saturation the queue extends past the zone, so the vehicles that wait longest are
precisely the ones the controller cannot count. This is a real limitation of camera-based control with a fixed field of view, and the strongest argument
here for a wider or second camera at a junction expected to saturate. At light and moderate load, where the queue sits inside the zone, the tail is
shortest under \relay{} (\SumoLowPNinetyFiveRelay~s against \SumoLowPNinetyFiveWebster~s at the lightest load).

The SUMO corridor (Experiment 9, supplementary Table~S7) settles the coupling question the point-queue
corridor left open. With links short enough
that a downstream queue physically blocks an upstream discharge, disabling the coupling term changes mean time
loss by \ScorrLowRedIsolated\%, \ScorrMidRedIsolated\% and \ScorrHighRedIsolated\% at the three levels, at most
\ScorrCoupleAbsMax\%. The corridor gain over the coordinated Webster plan, \ScorrRedCoordMin\% to
\ScorrRedCoordMax\%, is therefore attributable to local adaptivity alone in both traffic models; the aggregate downstream queue is too weak a proxy for the movement-specific pressure difference to add anything measurable, and the design would lose nothing by dropping it.

\subsection{Sensitivity}
\label{sec:sensitivity}

Experiment 10 (supplementary Fig.~S7) gives the two sensitivities. The fairness bound $R$ does not move the
mean at all on the single-dominant shape: from 30~s to unbounded, mean delay stays between \SensMidMeanMin{}
and \SensMidMeanMax~s at \SensMidDemand~veh/h and between \SensHighMeanMin{} and \SensHighMeanMax~s at
\SensHighDemand~veh/h, with the reduction against Webster between \SensHighRedMin\% and \SensHighRedMax\% at
the higher load, and the worst wait is likewise flat, \SensMidMaxWaitMin{} to \SensMidMaxWaitMax~s, because
at this shape the measured-cycle floor, not the starvation rule, determines when the minor approach is
served; $R$ binds where the score would otherwise leave a small queue unserved, the light-load case of
Section~\ref{sec:single}. The motorcycle weight, with two-wheeler shares that differ by axis, changes the reduction against Webster by at most \SensMotoSpread{} percentage points on any of the three
shapes (on the asymmetric shape from \SensMotoAsymmetricRedMin\% to \SensMotoAsymmetricRedMax\%); the controller degrades gracefully because it compares phases rather than reading absolute volumes and
the measured-cycle floor is a ratio in which a per-class weight partly cancels.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\textbf{This is not a field study.} No signal was controlled, there is no cabinet in the loop and no
before-and-after measurement, so the failure modes that appear only in the field (power interruption, lens contamination, camera
repositioning, seasonal light, drivers who learn that the signal responds to them) are untested.

\textbf{The fairness bound applies to the visible queue.} Experiment 8 shows that at saturation the vehicles
waiting longest are beyond the detection zone. Any camera-based controller shares this, and it should be
stated to an authority as a condition on camera placement rather than hidden in a mean.

\textbf{Perception was not validated against human ground truth.} The held-out split mixes synthetic frames
with pseudo-labelled real frames, so mAP measures agreement with the labelling process; Experiment 6b bounds
the consequence for control, but the magnitude of the undercount on the target city's junctions is unknown
and a human-annotated set is required before any absolute demand claim can be made.

\textbf{Narrower gaps.} The microsimulation uses SUMO defaults and a fleet plausible for the region rather
than one fitted to a Kathmandu junction, so it establishes that the advantage survives car-following, lane
changing, turning conflicts and spillback, not its magnitude at any site. The evaluated junctions have two
stages, so movement-level phasing and protected turns are generated but not exercised; a learned policy such
as PressLight~\cite{wei2019presslight} is not compared; the pedestrian logic is asserted by executable
invariants but never compared against a conventional pedestrian stage; and the corridor coupling term should
be removed or replaced by a movement-level pressure difference.

\section{Conclusion}

We have described a single-camera adaptive signal controller for heterogeneous urban traffic and evaluated it
in a seeded point-queue simulation, in SUMO with a motorcycle-dominated fleet, and on real fixed-camera
footage. Its distinguishing feature is that it measures the
junction rather than being told about it: arrival and discharge rates estimated from the same counts the
detector produces size each green from Webster's cycle on the measured degree of saturation and set the margin
a competing phase must clear.

Against a fixed plan granted the true mean demand the controller reduces mean delay in every cell of the
primary grid and in every one of the \GridCells{} cells of the robustness grid, the weakest of them near
parity beyond capacity; it beats plain max-pressure and self-organising control everywhere, and vehicle-actuated
control in \PolActuatedWins{} of \GridCells{} cells with a markedly shorter tail. In SUMO it reduces time loss
in every paired run against Webster, max-pressure and actuated control, by \SumoWebsterMin\% to
\SumoWebsterMax\% against the Webster plan, while its worst-case time loss at saturation is longer than under either that plan or actuated control, because the fairness bound reaches only as far as the camera sees. Two negative results are reported: the corridor coupling term changes delay by at most
\ScorrCoupleAbsMax\% in either traffic model, and an explanation we first offered for
the one weak cell was wrong and is replaced by a measured one. The next steps follow from the limitations: a
human-annotated perception set from the target city, a comparison against a learned policy on identical
arrivals, and a field trial.
