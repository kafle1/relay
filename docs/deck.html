Project R.E.L.A.Y.02
The objective

Three goals, all measurable

Cut waiting by a third or more at signalized junctions. Independent studies of Kathmandu say 33 to 49% is achievable; our simulation measures 44%.

Give ambulances a clear path through every junction on their route, automatically.

Keep it cheap enough that the city owns it. Ordinary hardware, our own software, no dependence on a foreign vendor.

Project R.E.L.A.Y.08
Cost, here in Nepal

Why ours costs less

where the money goes today

Lalitpur's junctions get new detector sensors, plate-reading cameras and a central server installed by a contractor, with a bundled 5-year maintenance contract.

We reuse the camera already on the pole and add one small computer. The intelligence is software, not new hardware.

No road is dug up, no new poles, no central server needed. One junction can run alone.

the numbers, all from Nepal

Rs 9 crore: Lalitpur's 2022 budget for 6 smart crossroads plus 1,100 street lights, bundled.

Rs 1.4 to 1.9 crore per junction: what the city's earlier signal plans priced (2016 and 2018 programmes).

Rs 1 to 2 lakh per junction, our estimate: the computer (Rs 50,000), signal interface, backup power, connectivity and fitting. A camera added where none exists.

Lalitpur's exact per-junction cost is not public. Ours is an estimate for equipment and fitting; support comes on top. Even at double it, the gap stays enormous.

Project R.E.L.A.Y.09
Business model

Who pays, and for what

The customer is the municipality. Lalitpur already proved a Nepali city will buy adaptive signals.

One-time setup per junction, then a yearly software and support subscription. The city owns the hardware outright.

Growth is junction by junction. Lalitpur's own feasibility study flagged 117 locations in that city alone.

Why a city picks us over a contractor system: cheaper hardware, no central server to keep alive, results on a public dashboard, and software the city can audit.

The subscription replaces the vendor maintenance contract. That contract lapsing is the exact thing that historically killed Kathmandu's lights.

Project R.E.L.A.Y.10
Safety

What happens when
something goes wrong

If the camera fails, the junction goes back to a normal fixed timer.

13 safety rules are checked by the software itself. Crossing greens are impossible.

85 failure situations are documented and handled in the code.

It runs in watch-only mode first. It controls nothing until the logs prove it works.

Traffic police keep a manual override at all times.

The AI only counts vehicles. The signal decision comes from readable math.

Project R.E.L.A.Y.10
Honest assessment

Strengths and weaknesses

strengths

✓ Hardware costs a few hundred dollars, not tens of thousands.

✓ Serves vehicles, pedestrians and ambulances.

✓ Every number comes from a test that can be rerun.

✓ At worst, it behaves like today's fixed timer.

weaknesses, and what we do about them

✕ Night and heavy rain reduce camera accuracy. We train on that footage, and the fallback is the fixed timer.

✕ An unusual camera angle can lower accuracy at first. The system keeps learning from its own footage, automatically.

✕ In total gridlock, no signal system helps. Below that point, the numbers above hold.

✕ Kathmandu's old lights died from poor maintenance. We use ordinary parts and remote monitoring, and start in watch-only mode.
