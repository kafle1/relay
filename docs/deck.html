What we built

The light follows.

CountEach road gets a score. A bus counts more than a bike.

3
DecideThe road with the highest score gets the green.
→
4
SwitchYellow first, then all red for a moment, then the next green.

Ambulances get green before they arrive, for a limited time.

Pedestrians are counted too. The walk light stays on while someone is crossing.

The decision logic is one page of published math. Anyone can check it.

fixed rules that always apply

No one waits more than 90 seconds.

An ambulance goes first, for a limited time.

Every green lasts 5 to 45 seconds.

When a lane empties, the green moves on.

R.E.L.A.Y.09
Safety

What happens when
something goes wrong

If the camera fails, the junction goes back to a normal fixed timer.

13 safety rules are checked by the software itself. Crossing greens are impossible.

85 failure situations are documented and handled in the code.

It runs in watch-only mode first. It controls nothing until the logs prove it works.

Traffic police keep a manual override at all times.

The AI only counts vehicles. The signal decision comes from readable math.

R.E.L.A.Y.10
Honest assessment

Strengths and weaknesses

strengths

✓ Hardware costs a few hundred dollars, not tens of thousands.

✓ Serves vehicles, pedestrians and ambulances.

✓ Every number here comes from a test we can rerun in front of you.

✓ At worst, it behaves like today's fixed timer.

weaknesses, and what we do about them

✕ Night and heavy rain reduce camera accuracy. We train on that footage, and the fallback is the fixed timer.

✕ An unusual camera angle can lower accuracy at first. The system keeps learning from its own footage, automatically.

✕ In total gridlock, no signal system helps. Below that point, the numbers above hold.

✕ Kathmandu's old lights died from poor maintenance. We use ordinary parts and remote monitoring, and start in watch-only mode.

The plan for Kathmandu
